## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Whether the supplier is in **breach** for (a) delivering the first batch three weeks late and (b) supplying 7,000 leaking containers

**Issue:**  
Whether the late arrival and leaking containers amount to breach of the sale contract.

**Rule:**  
Under Hong Kong sale of goods principles, if a seller fails to deliver in accordance with the contract, the buyer may claim damages for the loss directly and naturally resulting from the breach. Where goods are delivered late and accepted, damages are assessed by reference to the loss caused by the delay rather than simply by comparing contract price and market price: Sale of Goods Ordinance (Cap. 26), s 53(2). For non-delivery, the ordinary prima facie measure is the difference between the contract price and the market price at the delivery date, where an available market exists: Sale of Goods Ordinance (Cap. 26), s 53(3). A buyer must also take reasonable steps to mitigate loss, including obtaining substitute goods where appropriate: *ZHAOQING KIDSTAR FOODS LTD v. UTILITY INDUSTRIAL LTD* 2018 HKCFI 2668.

Separately, goods that leak are likely to be non-conforming goods. On ordinary Hong Kong contract and sale principles, supplying defective goods is a breach unless the contract validly narrows the seller’s responsibility.

**Application:**  
On the facts, there are two distinct breaches:

- **Late delivery:** the first batch arrived three weeks after the promised timing linked to your supermarket launch. If that delivery timing formed part of the contract, the delay is a straightforward breach. Even if the date was not an express contractual term, there may still be a breach if the contract, properly construed with the surrounding communications, required delivery in time for the launch.
- **Defective goods:** 7,000 containers leaked when filled. On its face, that is a failure to supply containers fit for their intended commercial use as food containers. Unless the contract validly excludes or limits responsibility, that is also a breach.

Because you continued trading and bought emergency replacements, your conduct is consistent with mitigation rather than affirming that no breach occurred.

**Conclusion:**  
There is a strong basis to say the supplier breached the contract by supplying leaking containers. There is also a potentially strong late-delivery claim, but its strength depends on whether the launch-date promise is legally part of the bargain.

### 2. What legal status the sales director’s **email promise** about arrival before the supermarket launch is likely to have, given that the signed contract does not state that date

**Issue:**  
Whether the email promise is likely to be legally binding despite the signed contract being silent on the date.

**Rule:**  
Whether a communication forms part of a contract is assessed objectively: the question is what a reasonable businessperson would understand from the parties’ exchanges and agreement. Hong Kong authority recognises that contractual terms may be established through emails and commercial communications where, viewed objectively, they form part of the parties’ agreement: *SCHENKER INTERNATIONAL (H.K.) LIMITED v. NATURAL DAIRY (NZ) HOLDINGS LIMITED* 2017 HKCFI 653.

However, where parties later sign a written contract that does not include an earlier statement, the written contract may be treated as the definitive record of their obligations, especially if it appears complete on its face. An earlier statement may still matter if it can properly be characterised as:
- an express contractual term incorporated into the agreement;
- a **collateral warranty** supporting the main contract; or
- a **representation**, potentially relevant to misrepresentation or construction.

A clause modifying or restricting ordinary remedies must be clear and unambiguous to be effective: Sale of Goods Ordinance (Cap. 26), s 57.

**Application:**  
The email promise helps you, particularly because it referred to a specific commercial purpose: arrival before the supermarket launch. That tends to show the supplier knew timing mattered. But there are evidential and construction difficulties because the signed contract does not mention that date.

The likely position is:

- If the contract was signed **after** the email and appears to be a complete written agreement, the supplier will argue the written contract superseded the email on timing.
- If the email was part of the agreed bargain and the signed contract was not intended to be exhaustive, you can argue the promise remained an express term.
- Even if not a main contractual term, the email may still support an argument that delivery before launch was a **collateral promise** or at least shows the supplier knew the commercial consequences of delay, which is relevant to remoteness and foreseeability of loss.
- If the signed contract contains an **entire agreement**, **non-reliance**, or similar clause, that would materially weaken reliance on the email. You have not mentioned one, so I assume none for now.

**Conclusion:**  
The email promise is not automatically displaced just because the signed contract is silent, but proving it as a binding delivery term is materially harder once a formal written contract omits the date. At minimum, the email is still likely to assist on **foreseeability of loss** and the commercial importance of timely delivery.

### 3. Whether the contractual terms requiring **defect notification within seven days**, **capping liability at the price of the affected batch**, and **excluding lost profits and customer claims** are likely to restrict your claim

**Issue:**  
Whether those three clauses are likely enforceable and effective against your claim.

**Rule:**  
Hong Kong law generally allows parties to agree contractual limits on damages, and if a contract fixes or modifies damages, the court will usually give effect to that agreement unless it is legally objectionable, including as a penalty in the appropriate context: Sale of Goods Ordinance (Cap. 26), s 57. Any clause that seeks to alter the buyer’s ordinary damages rights must be expressed clearly and unambiguously. The effect of such clauses is also limited in some contracts by the Control of Exemption Clauses Ordinance (Cap. 71), s 8.

As a matter of general Hong Kong contract principle, exclusion and limitation clauses are construed strictly. A party seeking to rely on them must show that the clause was incorporated into the contract and, on its proper wording, clearly covers the loss in question.

**Application:**  

**(a) Seven-day defect notice clause**  
This clause is a real risk to your defective-goods claim, but it is not necessarily decisive.

Points in your favour:
- The leaks allegedly appeared only when the containers were filled. That suggests a **latent defect**, not something reasonably discoverable on simple receipt inspection.
- If the clause is ambiguous as to whether the seven days runs from delivery or from reasonable discovery of the defect, a court may prefer the narrower construction against the supplier.
- If the clause is relied on to defeat liability for latent defects that could not reasonably be detected within seven days, statutory control under the Control of Exemption Clauses Ordinance may become relevant, though the exact outcome depends on the clause wording and the contract context.

Points in the supplier’s favour:
- You reported on day ten, not day seven.
- If the clause clearly requires notice within seven days of delivery regardless of discoverability, the supplier will argue the claim is contractually barred.

So the clause is a significant defence point, but the latent nature of the defect gives you a credible argument against a strict bar.

**(b) Liability cap at the price of the affected batch**  
If clearly drafted and properly incorporated, a batch-price cap is the kind of clause Hong Kong courts may in principle enforce. It is more likely to be effective for direct contractual claims than a vague or poorly drafted cap.

Key uncertainty: what counts as the **“affected batch”**?
- the entire first monthly batch; or
- only the 7,000 defective containers within it.

If the wording is unclear, that ambiguity may be construed against the supplier. The commercial result may differ substantially depending on the batch price.

**(c) Exclusion of lost profits and customer claims**  
This clause is likely to be the supplier’s strongest defence to consequential heads of loss.

- **Lost profits:** if your claim includes margin lost from supermarket disruption, the exclusion is likely aimed directly at that head of loss.
- **Customer claims:** the HK$320,000 charge from the supermarket may fall squarely within this wording if it is a charge-back, indemnity claim, or contractual debit imposed by your customer.
- However, the supplier must still show the clause clearly covers the particular payment. If the HK$320,000 is better characterised as a direct cost of your own performance rather than a third-party claim, there may be room to argue it is not excluded.

**Conclusion:**  
The contractual restrictions matter and may substantially reduce recovery. The supplier has a plausible defence based on all three clauses. Your best counterarguments are:
- the leakage was latent and not reasonably discoverable within seven days;
- any ambiguous clause should be construed narrowly;
- statutory control may limit the effect of exclusion and limitation clauses in this context.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Whether you may have a misrepresentation and/or contractual claim against the seller based on the statement that the largest customer had renewed for three years

**Issue:** Whether the seller is liable if the business was sold on the basis that its largest customer had renewed for three years, when the seller in fact held only an unsigned draft and the customer left shortly after completion.

**Rule:** Under Hong Kong law, a seller may be liable if it made a false statement of existing fact which induced the buyer to enter the transaction. If the statement was incorporated into the sale and purchase agreement, the buyer may also have a contractual claim for breach of warranty or breach of an express term. A statement that a key customer “had renewed for three years” is ordinarily a statement of present fact, not mere opinion, if it conveys that a concluded renewal existed. A claim is usually stronger if the seller knew the true position, or at least had no reasonable basis to say that a renewal had been secured. Hong Kong authority recognises the basic requirement that misrepresentation involves a false representation made to induce entry into the agreement: *Li Li Hong v Kilmorey International Holdings Limited* 2020 HKCFI 372.

**Application:** On your facts, there is an arguable case that the seller represented that the company had already secured a three-year renewal from its largest customer. If the real position was only an unsigned draft, that representation was at least arguably false. The point becomes materially stronger if:
- the seller said this in writing, especially in the SPA, disclosure bundle, management presentations, due diligence responses, or completion accounts papers;
- the seller knew there was no binding renewal; or
- the seller withheld the fact that the document was unsigned or still under negotiation.

You may also have a contractual claim if the SPA contained:
- a warranty that major contracts were in full force,
- a warranty that information supplied was true, accurate and not misleading,
- a warranty that there had been no material adverse change,
- a warranty identifying the largest customer relationship or revenue concentration, or
- an indemnity tied to inaccurate disclosures.

If the statement appeared only in negotiations and not in the SPA, the claim may still exist in misrepresentation, though the wording of the SPA will matter. Entire agreement, non-reliance, and warranty limitation clauses may affect the position.

The customer’s departure two months after completion does not itself prove the representation was false, but it is consistent with your case if the real complaint is that there was never a concluded renewal at all.

**Conclusion:** You likely have an arguable claim against the seller, especially in misrepresentation and potentially for breach of warranty, if you can show that the seller represented a concluded three-year renewal when only an unsigned draft existed and that this mattered to your decision and price.

### 2. Whether your own due diligence is likely to defeat or reduce a claim against the seller

**Issue:** Whether the seller can avoid liability by saying you conducted your own due diligence.

**Rule:** Under Hong Kong law, a buyer’s due diligence does not automatically bar a claim for misrepresentation or breach of warranty. The key questions are whether the buyer in fact relied on the seller’s statement, whether the true position was disclosed clearly enough to negate reliance, and whether the contract allocates risk to the buyer. Hong Kong cases in share sale disputes recognise the practical importance of due diligence obligations and document production in sale transactions; where a party was obliged to provide specified documents and failed to do so, that failure may be a serious breach: *Team Eight Group Limited v Lo Yuk Yee* 2021 HKCFI 1010; *Fong Kin Wa v Li Hau Yi* 2023 HKCFI 765.

**Application:** The seller’s “you did your own due diligence” point is not, by itself, a complete answer. It helps the seller only if the evidence shows, for example, that:
- the unsigned status of the renewal draft was actually disclosed to you or your advisers before signing or completion;
- your due diligence materials clearly revealed that no binding renewal existed;
- the SPA contained effective non-reliance language stating that you did not rely on pre-contract statements; or
- you proceeded despite knowing that the supposed renewal was uncertain.

If, however, the seller positively represented that the renewal had already been secured, and your diligence was conducted against that background, your having conducted diligence does not necessarily negate reliance. A court will usually look closely at the actual documents exchanged and what was or was not disclosed.

**Conclusion:** Your own due diligence does not automatically defeat a claim. It becomes damaging only if the seller can show that the true position was disclosed or that the contract clearly shifted the risk to you.

### 3. Whether you may have a contractual, tortious negligence, or negligent misstatement claim against the accounting firm for reporting that the customer had renewed

**Issue:** Whether the accounting firm may be liable for stating that the largest customer had renewed for three years when it had only management information and no signed renewal.

**Rule:** Under Hong Kong law, an accounting firm may face liability in contract if it failed to exercise the reasonable skill and care required by its retainer, and in tort for professional negligence or negligent misstatement if it owed you a duty of care, breached that duty, and caused loss. Much depends on the scope of engagement: what exactly the accountants were retained to do, whether they were verifying customer contracts or merely compiling management information, whether they included reservations or qualifications, and whether the report was prepared for your reliance in deciding whether to buy the company.

If a professional simply repeats management information without verification, liability often turns on whether that limitation was clearly disclosed and whether, given the purpose of the engagement, reasonable care required independent checking of a point as significant as the company’s largest customer renewal.

**Application:** On your facts, there is an arguable claim against the accounting firm if:
- the report was prepared for your acquisition decision;
- the firm knew the largest customer was commercially critical;
- the report presented the renewal as an established fact rather than an unverified management assertion;
- reasonable professional work would have required checking the underlying contract or at least confirming its signed status; and
- you relied on that statement when deciding to proceed or in fixing the price.

The accountant’s defence is stronger if the retainer and report clearly stated that:
- it was not performing an audit of customer contracts,
- it was relying on management representations without independent verification,
- no assurance was given on legal validity or enforceability of contracts, and
- the report was for limited purposes only.

Even then, a clear disclaimer is not always decisive if the actual wording of the report positively conveyed a verified conclusion rather than a caveated summary.

**Conclusion:** You may have a plausible negligence / negligent misstatement claim against the accounting firm, but its strength will depend heavily on the precise retainer terms, disclaimers, report wording, and whether reasonable accountants in that engagement would have checked the signed renewal.

### 4. Whether the accountant’s liability cap at twice the HK$180,000 fee is likely to limit recovery

**Issue:** Whether the accountant can rely on a contractual cap limiting liability to HK$360,000.

**Rule:** Under Hong Kong law, a contractual limitation or exclusion clause may in principle limit a professional firm’s liability, but its enforceability depends on the contract wording, the scope of the clause, whether the claim falls within it, and whether any applicable statutory control over exemption clauses renders it unenforceable or restricts its effect. Clauses are generally construed strictly, especially where a professional seeks to avoid liability for its own negligence. A distinction is also commonly drawn between ordinary negligence and fraud or fraudulent misrepresentation.

**Application:** The cap may be effective if:
- the retainer was made directly with you or your acquisition vehicle;
- the cap clearly covered negligence, negligent misstatement, and contractual claims arising from the report;
- the report was supplied subject to that retainer; and
- the clause survives the relevant statutory controls.

The cap may be vulnerable if:
- the wording is ambiguous and does not clearly cover the kind of claim you bring;
- the report was addressed to and intended for reliance by a wider class than the retainer contemplates;
- the clause fails any statutory reasonableness control;
- the conduct goes beyond negligence into dishonesty or recklessness; or
- the accountants represented that they had done more verification than they actually had.

A practical point is that even if the cap is legally arguable, the accounting firm may still face pressure if the report language was unqualified and the underlying point was obviously central to valuation.

**Conclusion:** The liability cap is a serious obstacle, but not necessarily conclusive. Its effect will depend on the exact wording of the retainer and report, the statutory control applicable to professional exclusion clauses, and the character of the alleged wrongdoing.

### 5. What loss is in principle recoverable, and what valuation evidence would likely be needed to prove it

**Issue:** How your loss would likely be measured, and what evidence is needed to prove that the company was worth materially less without the supposed customer renewal.

**Rule:** In a transaction of this kind, the court will generally require proof of actual loss, not merely proof that you paid a high price. Hong Kong authority shows that a claimant cannot simply assert that it overpaid and recover the difference as a matter of impression; the loss must be proved by evidence: *Pang Nina Wai Kit v Woo Hon* 2018 HKCFI 2736. In contract claims, the ordinary starting point is to assess damages at the date the cause of action accrued, but the court may use another date, or admit later evidence, if that is needed to avoid injustice or to reflect the compensatory principle: *Li Li Hong v Kilmorey International Holdings Limited* 2020 HKCFI 372. Hong Kong authority also recognises that post-breach events may assist in valuing loss where they shed light on value at the relevant date rather than merely showing what happened later: *Philips Hong Kong Ltd v A-G of Hong Kong* [1993] 1 HKLR 269; see also *Bank of East Asia Ltd v Yip Chi Wai* [2011] 5 HKLRD 761.

**Application:** Your most likely valuation case is not simply “I paid HK$8 million and now think it was worth much less.” You will usually need to show the **difference between**:
1. the value of the company as represented (with a genuine three-year renewal from its largest customer), and
2. its true value at the transaction date (without such a renewal, or with only a speculative unsigned draft).

That usually requires forensic valuation evidence, likely including:
- the percentage of revenue, gross profit, and EBITDA attributable to the customer;
- whether the customer relationship was contractually locked in or terminable;
- customer concentration risk;
- margins on that account;
- replacement prospects;
- whether the customer’s departure two months later reflects the true position at completion;
- any contemporaneous business plans, board papers, and budgets based on that customer continuing;
- EBITDA multiple / earnings multiple evidence for comparable logistics businesses, adjusted for concentration risk;
- discounted cash flow analysis, if the business was small and the customer concentration was unusually acute;
- a “but for” valuation using the represented three-year renewal versus an “actual” valuation using no binding renewal;
- working capital effects, debtor recoverability, and any knock-on impact on banking covenants or solvency.

Later events can be useful evidence. For example, the customer’s departure two months after completion may support your case that there was no real secured renewal and that the represented value was inflated. But the valuation must still be anchored to the value at the acquisition date, not hindsight alone.

Depending on the cause of action, you may also need to analyse:
- whether you would have refused the deal entirely, or would have bought only at a lower price;
- what price reduction would have been negotiated had the truth been known;
- whether any part of the loss was caused by post-completion management decisions rather than the false statement.

**Conclusion:** You will need a proper valuation case based on diminution in value at the transaction date, supported by financial and industry evidence. A bare assertion that you overpaid will usually be insufficient.

3. **Build a reliance record**
   - Gather documents showing that the customer renewal mattered to price and deal decision: investment committee papers, lender papers, acquisition model, emails with advisers, marked-up SPA drafts, and any references to customer concentration.
   - If possible, identify whether the HK$8 million price assumed continuation of that customer.

4. **Instruct a forensic accountant / valuation expert early**
   - Ask for a preliminary view on:
     - revenue and earnings dependence on the customer;
     - value with a genuine 3-year renewal;
     - value without a binding renewal;
     - the effect of the actual customer loss two months later as evidence of value at completion;
     - whether the business had any realistic substitute customers.
   - The valuation should avoid a simplistic “price paid minus estimated true worth” approach without methodology.

5. **Assess claims separately against seller and accountant**
   - **Seller:** misrepresentation, breach of warranty, breach of contractual disclosure obligations, and possibly deceit if facts justify it.
   - **Accountant:** breach of contract, negligence, negligent misstatement, and challenge to the liability cap if appropriate.

6. **Obtain a privilege-protected merits review**
   - A solicitor should review:
     - the wording of the seller warranties and reliance provisions;
     - whether there is a contractual notice requirement for warranty claims;
     - whether the accountant’s cap and disclaimer are enforceable;
     - whether the facts support ordinary negligence only, or something more serious.

7. **Send carefully framed preservation / pre-action correspondence**
   - This should usually request preservation of working papers, management communications, and customer documents.
   - It should be drafted carefully so you do not unintentionally narrow your case before the evidence picture is complete.

### Risk assessment

- Your claim is **stronger** if the renewal was presented as an existing signed deal and was central to value.
- It is **weaker** if the documents show only that renewal was expected or under negotiation, or if your own diligence clearly revealed the absence of a signed contract.
- The accountant claim may be **commercially limited** by the liability cap unless the clause is vulnerable or the conduct is more serious than ordinary negligence.
- The biggest practical challenge is usually **proof of loss**, not merely proof that the statement was wrong.

### 1. Whether the directors’ conduct may amount to **breach of directors’ duties** under Hong Kong law

**Issue:**  
Whether the payment of management fees to a spouse-owned company, diversion of a major customer to a new business owned by the controllers, withholding management information, and a proposed dilutive share issue on terms refused to you may involve breach of duty by the directors.

**Rule:**  
Under Hong Kong company law, directors owe fiduciary and other duties to the company. As a general principle, they must act bona fide in the interests of the company, must not place themselves in a position of conflict, and must not make unauthorised profits from corporate opportunities or related transactions. A director also must not use corporate powers for an improper purpose.

The authorities provided support several aspects of that general position. In the derivative-action context, the court in *Yuen Wai Yee v Li Kwok Hung* [2021] HKCFI 2543 treated the **no-conflict rule**, the rule against **secret profits**, and the duty to act in the company’s interests as central where directors allegedly caused the company to enter into conflicted transactions and diverted value. In the unfair-prejudice context, the court has recognised that **misappropriation of company assets** and **diversion of corporate opportunity** can found relief, and that exercise of powers for an **improper purpose** may also be relevant under Companies Ordinance (Cap. 622), s 724: *Xu Liu Chun v Wu Chang Jiang* [2018] HKCFI 1575; *Chiu Wing Keung Mikey v Interasia Corporation Limited & Ors* [2025] HKCFI 386; Companies Ordinance (Cap. 622), s 724.

**Application:**  
On your facts, there are at least four substantial duty-risk areas:

- **HK$1.5 million management fees to a spouse-owned company:** this is a classic conflict situation. - **Transfer of a major customer to a new business owned by the same controllers:** that raises a serious **corporate opportunity** issue. If the customer belonged, or should have been available, to the Hong Kong company, moving it to another vehicle owned by the directors is potentially a diversion of company business for their own benefit.
- **Share issue designed to dilute you below 10% while rejecting your offer to subscribe on the same terms:** if the stated need for cash is genuine, a capital raise may be legitimate. But if they refused your matching offer and structured the issue mainly to reduce your influence or litigation leverage, that strongly suggests an **improper purpose** rather than a genuine financing exercise.
- **Stopping management accounts:** withholding information is not, by itself, always a standalone fiduciary breach owed to you personally as shareholder. But in context it may support an inference that the controllers are concealing self-interested conduct.

Much will turn on evidence: board minutes, invoices, service agreements, proof of work done by the spouse-owned company, customer communications, and the terms of the proposed allotment. On the facts you gave, there appears to be at least a **serious case** that the directors have preferred their own interests over the company’s.

**Conclusion:**  
Yes. On the present facts, there is a credible basis to allege breaches of directors’ duties, especially concerning **conflicted related-party payments**, **diversion of business**, and possibly the **proposed share issue for an improper purpose**.

### 2. Whether the matters described may amount to **unfairly prejudicial conduct** against you as a member

**Issue:**

**Rule:**  
The court may grant relief if a company’s affairs are being or have been conducted in a manner **unfairly prejudicial** to the interests of members or one or more members, or if an act or omission of the company is or would be so prejudicial: Companies Ordinance (Cap. 622), s 724. The remedy is deliberately broad and flexible. The court looks at the conduct **as a whole** and in context, rather than in isolation: *Chiu Wing Keung Mikey v Interasia Corporation Limited & Ors* [2025] HKCFI 386.

The cases provided support the following propositions:

- **Misappropriation of company assets** may amount to unfair prejudice: *Xu Liu Chun v Wu Chang Jiang* [2018] HKCFI 1575.
- **Diversion of corporate opportunity** may amount to unfair prejudice: Companies Ordinance (Cap. 622), s 724.
- **Departure from the parties’ agreed arrangements**, including shareholder bargains, is often the starting point in assessing unfairness: *Xu Liu Chun v Wu Chang Jiang* [2018] HKCFI 1575.
- In a small private company, **exclusion from management** contrary to the understanding on which the company was formed can be unfairly prejudicial: *Xu Liu Chun v Wu Chang Jiang* [2018] HKCFI 1575; *Chiu Wing Keung Mikey v Interasia Corporation Limited & Ors* [2025] HKCFI 386.
- The court has a wide discretion to grant relief, including a **buy-out order**, where that is fair: Companies Ordinance (Cap. 622), s 725; *Chiu Wing Keung Mikey v Interasia Corporation Limited & Ors* [2025] HKCFI 386.

**Application:**  
Your facts fit several recognised categories of unfair prejudice:

1. **Self-interested extraction of value**  
   Payment of large management fees to a company owned by a director’s spouse is capable of being characterised as misapplication of company funds, especially if your shareholders’ agreement required unanimous approval for related-party transactions and that was not obtained.

2. **Diversion of a major customer**  
   Moving an existing customer to the controllers’ new business appears to divert value away from the company in which you hold 25%. That is strongly consistent with unfair prejudice because it reduces the company’s value while benefiting those in control.

3. **Information denial**  
   Refusal to provide management accounts may support the inference that the controllers are acting oppressively or concealing misconduct. On its own it may not always justify relief, but as part of a wider pattern it is relevant.

4. **Dilutive share issue**  
   The proposed allotment appears especially important. If they reject your offer to invest on identical terms yet press ahead with an allotment that drops you below 10%, the court may infer that the real objective is not funding but **dilution**. That would be a strong indicator of unfairness, particularly if the dilution affects your practical ability to protect your position.

5. **Breach of the shareholder bargain**  
   Your agreement requires unanimous approval for related-party transactions. A deliberate departure from that agreed protection is the kind of conduct courts commonly treat as relevant to unfair prejudice.

Taken together, the conduct is stronger than a complaint about ordinary commercial disagreement. It looks like a pattern of controllers extracting value, excluding scrutiny, and using corporate machinery to weaken a minority holder.

**Conclusion:**  
Yes. On the facts provided, you appear to have a **substantial potential unfair prejudice claim** under Companies Ordinance (Cap. 622), s 724, with the proposed share issue likely to be the most urgent element.

### 3. Whether you may have a **contractual claim** under the shareholders’ agreement

**Issue:**  
Whether breach of the shareholders’ agreement gives you a separate claim in contract.

**Rule:**  
As a general Hong Kong principle, a shareholders’ agreement is enforceable according to its terms between its parties. If it requires **unanimous approval** for related-party transactions, a transaction entered into without that approval may amount to breach of contract. Depending on the wording, the affected shareholder may seek damages, a declaration, or injunctive relief.

The unfair-prejudice authorities also recognise that a **departure from the bargain between shareholders** can be central to the court’s assessment of unfairness: *Xu Liu Chun v Wu Chang Jiang* [2018] HKCFI 1575.

**Application:**  
If all relevant parties signed the shareholders’ agreement, the payment of management fees to a spouse-owned company without unanimous approval appears to be a direct contractual problem. Key points will be:

- whether “related-party transaction” is defined broadly enough to catch a spouse-owned entity;
- whether unanimous approval had to be given **before** the transaction;
- whether the agreement binds only the shareholders or also the company;
- whether the agreement restricts share allotments, pre-emption, funding rounds, or dilution;
- whether there is any dispute-resolution clause or exclusive forum clause.

If the agreement is silent on exits, that does **not** prevent relief for breach. It simply means the contract may not itself give you a clean buy-out mechanism.

The proposed share issue may also engage contract if the agreement contains:
- pre-emption rights,
- anti-dilution protections,
- reserved matters requiring unanimity,
- director-appointment or information-rights provisions, or
- good-faith or equal-treatment clauses.

**Conclusion:**  
You likely have a **separate contractual claim**, especially regarding the related-party payments, and possibly also regarding the proposed share issue if the agreement contains capital or consent protections beyond the clause you mentioned.

In small private-company disputes, a **buy-out order** is often the most suitable final remedy, especially where trust has broken down or the minority has been effectively marginalised: *Chiu Wing Keung Mikey v Interasia Corporation Limited & Ors* [2025] HKCFI 386. ## Legal Issues Identified

2. Whether the employer may lawfully **withhold HK$280,000 in commission** following that dismissal.
3. Your likely **confidentiality exposure**, including the risk of an **injunction**, arising from taking the spreadsheet and from joining a competitor.
4. Whether the **12-month Asia-wide non-compete** is likely to be enforceable in Hong Kong.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Whether the employer was likely entitled to **summarily dismiss** you for emailing the customer spreadsheet to your personal account

**Issue:**  
Whether your conduct was serious enough to justify immediate dismissal without notice or payment in lieu.

**Rule:**  
Under Hong Kong law, summary dismissal is only justified where the employee has committed conduct sufficiently serious to amount to repudiatory breach, gross misconduct, fraud, dishonesty, or conduct incompatible with the faithful discharge of duty. The statutory basis includes **Employment Ordinance (Cap. 57), s 9(1)(b)**. Hong Kong authorities also recognise that conduct destroying trust and confidence may justify immediate dismissal even without proved dishonesty. The burden is on the employer to justify the dismissal. A dismissal that does not meet that threshold is wrongful dismissal. These principles are reflected in *Hu Yangyong v. Alba Asia Ltd* [2025] HKCFI 2484 and in the recognised common law grounds discussed together with **Employment Ordinance (Cap. 57), s 9(1)(b)**.

**Application:**  
On your facts, the employer has an arguable case for serious misconduct because the spreadsheet appears to be **customer data**, which is commercially sensitive and commonly protected by confidentiality obligations. Emailing it to a personal account creates a real risk of loss of control over confidential information, even if you say the purpose was only to work from home.

That said, the present facts do **not automatically** establish grounds for summary dismissal. The key questions are:

- whether the spreadsheet was clearly confidential and access-restricted;
- whether there was an express policy forbidding transfer to personal email or personal devices;
- whether you had previously been permitted to work remotely in that way;
- whether the transfer was limited, temporary, and for genuine work purposes;
- whether there is evidence of onward disclosure, misuse, deletion failure, or intent to assist the competitor; and
- whether you had already accepted the competitor's offer before sending the spreadsheet.

If the employer can show that you transferred sensitive customer information shortly before leaving for a competitor, in breach of a clear policy, a court may regard that as conduct undermining trust and confidence sufficiently to justify summary dismissal. If, however, the evidence supports your explanation that this was a one-off act for work-at-home purposes, with no external disclosure, no misuse, and no dishonest intent, the employer may face difficulty proving that the conduct crossed the high threshold for summary dismissal rather than ordinary misconduct.

The fact that you **did not send it to anyone else** helps you, but it does not eliminate risk. Hong Kong courts can treat misuse or unauthorised retention of confidential business information as serious even without proven onward disclosure.

**Conclusion:**  
Based on the facts given, the employer has a **credible but not automatic** basis to argue summary dismissal. The outcome is likely to turn on the sensitivity of the spreadsheet, the employer's written policies, timing relative to your move to a competitor, and whether there is evidence of misuse beyond emailing it to yourself. If those aggravating facts are weak, the dismissal may be vulnerable as **wrongful dismissal**.

### 2. Whether the employer may lawfully **withhold HK$280,000 in commission** following that dismissal

**Issue:**  
Whether immediate dismissal allows the employer to refuse payment of accrued commission.

**Rule:**  
Even where employment is terminated, an employer is not generally free to withhold sums that are already contractually earned. If the summary dismissal was not justified, the termination is wrongful and the employee may claim the remuneration that should have been paid under the contract, subject to the contractual terms governing when commission is earned and payable. *Hu Yangyong v. Alba Asia Ltd* [2025] HKCFI 2484 shows that where summary dismissal is not established, the employee may recover contractual sums lost by the wrongful termination.

Whether commission is payable depends heavily on the contract terms, for example whether commission is earned on booking, invoicing, payment collection, continued employment on the payment date, or satisfaction of conditions precedent.

**Application:**  
The critical issue is not merely whether you were dismissed, but **whether the HK$280,000 had already accrued under the commission scheme** before termination.

Common possibilities are:

- **If commission had already been earned** under the contract or commission plan before dismissal, the employer usually cannot withhold it simply by labelling the dismissal as summary.
- **If commission was discretionary**, the employer has more room to resist payment, though discretion must still be exercised honestly and for proper purposes.
- **If commission was contingent** on remaining employed on a payment date, or on customer payment, shipment, installation, or other milestones not yet met, the employer may argue it never became due.
- **If the employer argues forfeiture for misconduct**, that will depend on clear contractual wording and whether the misconduct truly triggers the clause.

So the contract wording is central. If the commission relates to deals already closed and all contractual triggers were met before dismissal, your claim is materially stronger. If the plan says no commission is payable after termination, or only to employees still employed when the company receives payment, your position becomes weaker, though such clauses must still be construed carefully.

**Conclusion:**  
The employer is **not automatically entitled** to withhold the HK$280,000 because it dismissed you summarily. Your entitlement depends primarily on **when commission was earned under the contract or commission plan**, and secondarily on whether the summary dismissal was justified. If the sums had already accrued, you likely have a substantial claim for payment.

### 3. Your likely **confidentiality exposure**, including the risk of an **injunction**, arising from taking the spreadsheet and from joining a competitor

**Issue:**  
Whether the employer is likely to succeed in confidentiality-based claims or obtain an injunction against you.

**Rule:**  
Employees owe duties of confidentiality in respect of genuinely confidential information acquired during employment. Hong Kong courts may grant injunctive relief to restrain misuse or threatened misuse of confidential information where there is a serious issue to be tried and damages would not be an adequate remedy. The court will look closely at the nature of the information, whether it is truly confidential, whether it was removed or retained without authority, and whether there is evidence of actual or threatened misuse. These points are illustrated by *Greater China Appraisal Ltd v Tsang Kang Po and O* [2018] HKCFI 2552. Secondary guidance also recognises that not all information useful to a rival is legally confidential.

**Application:**  
A **customer spreadsheet** in a medical-device business may well contain confidential material, especially if it includes any of the following:

- customer identities not publicly available;
- purchasing history;
- pricing or discount structures;
- margins;
- decision-maker contacts;
- pipeline data;
- tender history; or
- strategic notes.

If the spreadsheet contains only basic customer names already well known in the market, the employer's case is weaker. If it contains detailed commercial intelligence, the risk is substantially higher.

The employer's threat of an injunction is more credible if it can show one or more of these facts:

- you still possess the spreadsheet or backup copies;
- the data was sent shortly before resignation or while negotiating with a competitor;
- the competitor operates in the same customer segment or territory;
- you have not confirmed deletion and non-use;
- you contacted those customers using knowledge derived from the spreadsheet; or
- there is evidence of downloading, printing, forwarding, or further retention.

On your current facts, the employer may well have enough to seek a **protective confidentiality injunction**, especially one requiring you not to use, copy, disclose, or retain the spreadsheet. However, a broader injunction stopping you from working for the competitor altogether would usually require more than the mere act of emailing the spreadsheet to yourself.

A court will also distinguish between:
- restraining misuse of **confidential information**, which is relatively more achievable for the employer; and
- restraining you from taking up employment generally, which usually requires reliance on a valid restraint covenant.

If you no longer hold the spreadsheet, never forwarded it, and can provide prompt undertakings to delete and not use it, that may reduce the scope of any injunction and the employer's urgency argument.

**Conclusion:**  
Your **confidentiality risk is real**. The employer has a plausible basis to seek an injunction preventing use or disclosure of the spreadsheet, particularly if it contains commercially sensitive customer intelligence. The employer's position is materially stronger on a **narrow confidentiality injunction** than on a broad attempt to stop you working for the competitor.

### 4. Whether the **12-month Asia-wide non-compete** is likely to be enforceable in Hong Kong

**Issue:**  
Whether the post-termination restraint is likely to be upheld.

**Rule:**  
Under Hong Kong law, a post-termination non-compete is enforceable only to the extent that it goes no further than is reasonably necessary to protect a **legitimate business interest**, such as trade secrets, confidential information, or customer connections. A clause that is wider than necessary in duration, geography, scope of activities, or class of restricted customers is vulnerable as an unlawful restraint of trade. Secondary Hong Kong guidance also notes that non-compete provisions are open to challenge where there is no sufficient legitimate interest or the scope of restraint is excessive.

**Application:**  
On the facts given, a **12-month Asia-wide non-compete** is exposed to challenge for breadth.

Factors weighing **against enforceability**:

- **Geographic width:** “Asia-wide” is very broad unless your actual role genuinely covered that market in a meaningful way.
- **Duration:** 12 months is at the more onerous end, especially for an employee rather than a seller of a business.
- **Role-specific necessity:** If your actual customer responsibility was limited to particular territories, accounts, or product lines, an Asia-wide prohibition may exceed what is reasonably necessary.

- the medical-device sector may involve long sales cycles, tenders, pricing strategies, and relationship-driven business; and
- if your portfolio truly covered multiple Asian markets and key strategic accounts, a wider covenant becomes more defensible.

Even so, an **Asia-wide ban on working for a competitor for 12 months** often looks wider than necessary unless very carefully tailored. A court is generally more receptive to narrower restraints, such as restrictions limited to:
- customers you dealt with materially in the final 6–12 months,
- specific product lines,
- specific territories you actually managed, or
- non-solicitation and non-dealing obligations rather than a total employment ban.

The unlimited confidentiality clause is a separate matter. A confidentiality obligation may continue post-termination for so long as the information remains genuinely confidential. But if drafted to cover material that is not truly confidential, it will not necessarily be enforced to that full literal extent.

The **12-month Asia-wide non-compete is vulnerable and may well be unenforceable in its current breadth**, unless the employer can show that your role genuinely justified such a wide restraint and that narrower measures would not adequately protect its legitimate interests. By contrast, the **confidentiality obligations are more likely to be enforceable**, but only in relation to information that is truly confidential.

### 1. Who is likely to own the copyright in the booking platform code created by the freelance developer

**Issue:**  
Whether payment for a commissioned software project, without an express intellectual property assignment, makes your company the copyright owner of the code.

**Rule:**  
Under Hong Kong law, the starting position is that the **author** of a work is generally the copyright owner unless ownership has been transferred by agreement. In relation to a commissioned computer program, secondary Hong Kong legal guidance states that, where ownership has not been agreed, the freelance programmer remains the copyright owner, but the commissioning customer obtains an **exclusive licence** to exploit the program for the purposes that both parties could reasonably have contemplated when the program was commissioned. That guidance attributes this result to **Copyright Ordinance, s 15(2)**. It also indicates that the customer may restrain exploitation of the program for a purpose to which it can reasonably object, such as sale to a competitor. The court also distinguishes between protected **expression** and unprotected general ideas or concepts: copyright protects the particular form of expression, not the underlying idea: *Glowjoy Group Company Limited v Wong Wai Chun* 2019 HKCFI 2672.

**Application:**  
On the facts given, the developer appears to have been an **independent freelancer**, not your employee, and your emails describe deliverables and price but do **not expressly assign copyright**. On that assumption, your company is **less likely to be the copyright owner** of the platform code merely because it paid for the work.

However, your position is not limited to a bare right to use whatever the developer chooses to permit. If the platform was commissioned specifically for your company’s booking operations, there is a substantial basis to argue that your company has an **exclusive right to use and exploit the program** for the business purposes both sides reasonably contemplated when the platform was commissioned. If licensing substantially similar code to a direct competitor undermines that expected exclusivity, there is a credible argument that this goes beyond what the developer is entitled to do.

The strength of that argument will depend on facts such as:

- whether the platform was built specifically around your workflows, branding, customer journey, pricing logic or proprietary requirements;
- whether the emails suggest a **bespoke** rather than off-the-shelf solution;
- whether the competitor’s code is in substance the same codebase or merely shares general functionality; and
- whether exclusivity can reasonably be inferred from the nature of the commission.

Because copyright protects expression rather than ideas, the developer is not prevented from using general know-how, generic coding techniques, or abstract booking-platform concepts. The more the competitor product reproduces your commissioned code, structure, architecture, databases, screens, or other original expressive features, the stronger your position.

**Conclusion:**  
On the present facts, the developer is **more likely than not to remain the copyright owner** of the code unless there is an express or clearly implied assignment. But your company is likely to have a **strong argument for an exclusive licence or equivalent exclusive exploitation right** for the commissioned platform within the scope reasonably contemplated at the time of commissioning, and that may support steps to stop licensing the same or substantially similar code to a competitor.

### 2. Who is likely to own the copyright in the logo, and whether the developer’s use of the same logo concept for a competitor is likely to infringe

**Issue:**  
Whether your company owns the copyright in the logo despite no express assignment, and whether reuse of the same logo concept for a competitor is likely to amount to infringement.

**Rule:**  
The basic ownership position under Hong Kong copyright law is that, absent an agreement transferring ownership, the **author** ordinarily owns the copyright. Copyright protects the **particular expression** of a work, not the underlying idea or concept: *Glowjoy Group Company Limited v Wong Wai Chun* 2019 HKCFI 2672. Whether copying has occurred is a factual question. Relevant circumstances include likely access, objective similarities, and whether the similarities are sufficiently distinctive to support an inference of copying. Whether a substantial part has been taken is judged impressionistically by comparing the works and considering the quality of what was taken rather than only the amount: *Morn Creations Ltd v Swire Resources Ltd* 2018 HKCFI 179. A defendant cannot avoid copyright liability merely by using the copied work in another legal form or context: *OWNDAYS Co., Ltd v Professional Optometrist Li* 2019 HKCFI 3147.

**Application:**  
If the logo was created by the freelancer and there was **no express assignment**, your company is again **not automatically the copyright owner** merely because it paid for the design work. On the present facts, the developer is therefore likely to argue, with some force, that he owns the copyright in the logo artwork.

That said, his reference to the “same logo concept” matters. If what he is licensing is only a **general idea, theme, or style**, that may fall outside copyright protection. But if the competitor’s logo reproduces your logo’s **actual artistic expression** or a substantial part of it, there is a stronger infringement case.

The practical question is therefore not only ownership, but also **what exactly has been reused**:

- If the competitor logo is identical or very close to your logo artwork, there is a stronger inference of copying.
- If it uses the same arrangement, shapes, stylisation, and visual features, that may still amount to reproduction of a substantial part.
- If it only adopts a broadly similar concept or design brief, your copyright case is weaker.

There may also be commercial confusion issues outside pure copyright analysis, but on the facts provided and the authorities available, the clearest copyright point is that **idea-level similarity is not enough**; the dispute turns on whether the competitor’s logo reproduces protected expression.

**Conclusion:**  
Without an express assignment, the developer is **likely to be treated as the copyright owner** of the logo artwork. But if the competitor’s logo copies the **actual artistic expression** of your logo rather than just its concept, there may still be a basis to challenge the reuse depending on the exact facts and on any contractual or licence restrictions that can be inferred from the commission.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Whether the incident is likely to engage your data security obligations under Hong Kong law

**Issue:** Whether the apparent ransomware intrusion and possible exfiltration of customer data are likely to engage your legal duties as a data user.

**Rule:** In Hong Kong, there is no single standalone cybercrime or cybersecurity statute. Computer-related misconduct is addressed through a number of existing ordinances, while personal-data protection obligations are governed separately under the **Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (Cap. 486)**. For data security, the central obligation is that a data user must take **all practicable steps** to protect personal data against unauthorised or accidental access, processing, erasure, loss or use, having regard to factors such as the sensitivity of the data, the harm that may result, where the data is stored, the security built into the relevant systems, the reliability of persons with access, and the security of transmission. Where a data processor is engaged, the data user must also use contractual or other means to prevent unauthorised or accidental access, processing, erasure, loss or use of the transferred data. A data breach does **not automatically** mean the Ordinance has been breached; breach depends on whether the Privacy Commissioner concludes, after investigation, that the data user failed to take all practicable steps. A breach of the security principle does not itself constitute an offence, but non-compliance with an enforcement notice can become an offence: Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (Cap. 486), Sch 1, Principle 4.

**Application:** On your facts, the affected dataset appears highly sensitive. Names, HKID numbers, addresses, telephone numbers, order histories, and password hashes together create a substantial risk of identity theft, account compromise, phishing, and targeted fraud. That materially increases the level of protection expected. The fact pattern also points to two possible control failures:

- an administrator account was allegedly accessed through **password reuse** by your employee; and
- the cloud vendor allegedly did **not enforce multi-factor authentication** despite representing that it would.

Those facts do not yet determine liability, but they are directly relevant to whether **all practicable steps** were taken. The Commissioner would likely scrutinise, among other things:

- password management policies;
- privileged access controls;
- whether MFA was required and actually enforced;
- vendor due diligence and contractual controls;
- access logging and monitoring;
- segregation of duties and least-privilege controls;
- encryption and password-hash strength;
- incident response speed; and
- whether personal data held was broader than operationally necessary.

Because you restored from backups, operational recovery has begun, but restoration does not answer whether the original controls were adequate or whether data was copied out. The fact that the attacker supplied a sample strongly suggests at least some exfiltration has already occurred.

**Conclusion:** Yes. The incident plainly engages your obligations under **DPP4**. Whether you are in breach depends on the facts established on investigation, especially the adequacy of your internal access controls and your contractual and practical controls over the cloud vendor.

### 2. Whether you are under a legal obligation to notify affected customers or the Privacy Commissioner, and what the practical position is

**Issue:** Whether Hong Kong law requires notification of the data breach to the Privacy Commissioner or affected customers.

**Rule:** Under Hong Kong law, a data breach does **not** trigger a general mandatory notification requirement to either the Privacy Commissioner or affected data subjects. However, the Commissioner strongly recommends notification where there is a reasonably foreseeable real risk of harm to affected individuals. The absence of a mandatory reporting rule does not remove the underlying duty to take all practicable protective steps under the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (Cap. 486), Sch 1, Principle 4, and the Commissioner may investigate whether that duty was met. Guidance recommending breach notification is persuasive regulatory guidance rather than primary legislation.

**Application:** Here, the risk of harm appears significant. The compromised data categories include HKID numbers and contact details, and there is an express threat to publish within 48 hours. Even if you do not yet know whether every record was copied, the sample and threat make the risk concrete rather than speculative. In those circumstances, although notification is not legally mandatory in the ordinary sense, prompt notification would usually be the prudent course because:

- affected customers may need to change passwords, watch for impersonation and phishing, and protect their accounts;
- early engagement with the Commissioner tends to be more defensible than delay where serious harm is reasonably foreseeable; and
- your eventual position will be stronger if you can show timely, risk-based action rather than waiting for perfect forensic certainty.

Because password hashes were affected, the urgency of customer-facing password reset measures depends on the strength of the hashing and whether the same credentials may be used elsewhere. But from a risk perspective, it would usually be safer to assume possible misuse.

**Conclusion:** There is no general statutory duty automatically requiring notification, but on these facts prompt notification to the Privacy Commissioner and affected customers is strongly advisable.

### 3. What powers the Privacy Commissioner may use if the attacker threatens to publish the data online

**Issue:** Whether the threatened online publication can be addressed through the Privacy Commissioner’s cessation-notice powers.

**Rule:** The Privacy Commissioner may issue a **cessation notice** requiring a Hong Kong person or non-Hong Kong service provider to take specified steps to cease or restrict a qualifying disclosure of personal data of a Hong Kong resident or person present in Hong Kong, where the disclosure was made without the relevant consent and the relevant intent or recklessness requirement is met. In the online context, cessation action can include removing the message from the platform, restricting access to it, or discontinuing relevant hosting services: Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (Cap. 486), ss 66K and 66L.

**Application:** If the attacker posts or starts distributing the dataset online, those powers may become practically important. The customer base appears to include Hong Kong residents, and the threatened disclosure would likely be without consent. If the relevant mental element is present, the Commissioner may seek to stop or restrict publication through platform or hosting intermediaries, including where service providers are outside Hong Kong but fall within the statutory reach described in the Ordinance.

That said, these powers are generally more useful **once disclosure is occurring or imminent on an identifiable platform**. They are not a substitute for your own immediate steps to preserve evidence, alert platforms or hosts as soon as publication appears, and communicate with the Commissioner urgently if any leak URL, channel, or hosting point is identified.

**Conclusion:** Yes. If publication occurs or identifiable publication is imminent, the Commissioner’s cessation-notice regime may be an important tool to suppress or restrict further dissemination.

### 4. What potential civil, regulatory, and contractual liability may arise in relation to your business and the cloud vendor

**Issue:** What liability exposures may arise for your company and the vendor.

**Rule:** From a privacy-regulatory perspective, a data breach does not by itself establish liability. The key question is whether the data user failed to take all practicable security steps under **DPP4**. If the Commissioner finds non-compliance, the usual immediate regulatory mechanism is an enforcement notice requiring remedial action; failure to comply with that notice may become an offence. Separately, where a data user uses a data processor, the data user must use contractual or other means to protect transferred data: Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (Cap. 486), Sch 1, Principle 4. Beyond the Ordinance, liability between you and the vendor will depend materially on the service contract, any security commitments, limitation and indemnity clauses, and the factual allocation of responsibility. **Application:** Your exposure and the vendor’s exposure are likely to run on parallel tracks:

That means the Commissioner would ordinarily examine your controls first, including employee credential management, privileged-access restrictions, and vendor oversight.
- **Cloud vendor:** If the vendor acted as a processor or infrastructure provider on your behalf, its failures may still be highly relevant because DPP4 expects you to use contractual or other means to protect transferred data. If the vendor promised MFA in its proposal but failed to implement it, that may support a contractual claim, misrepresentation arguments, or a defence to any attempt by the vendor to place full responsibility on you. But the legal effect depends on whether the proposal was incorporated into the binding contract and how responsibility was allocated in the final terms.
- **Shared-fault scenario:** On the present facts, a regulator or court could potentially conclude that both sides contributed: your employee’s password reuse may indicate an internal control weakness, while the vendor’s alleged failure to enforce MFA may indicate service-delivery or contractual breach on its side.

You also face reputational and commercial exposure from customer complaints, possible claims, payment-provider concerns, and costs of remediation even if formal regulatory liability is not ultimately established.

**Conclusion:** Potential liability exists for both your business and the vendor, but the immediate regulatory focus is likely to be whether **you**, as data user, took all practicable steps and properly controlled your processor relationship. The vendor may separately face contractual exposure if its MFA commitment formed part of the binding deal or service standard.

### 1. Whether the leaks and temporary closures are likely to suspend or reduce the rent payable under a Hong Kong commercial lease

**Issue:**  
Whether repeated leaks from the flat above, causing two closures of your restaurant, are likely to trigger rent suspension or abatement.

**Rule:**  
Under Hong Kong law, rent generally continues to be payable even if premises become less usable or even unfit for the tenant’s intended use, unless the lease provides otherwise. A rent suspension or abatement right usually depends on an **express lease clause**. Where such a clause exists, it commonly requires **serious physical destruction or damage** to the premises, caused without the tenant’s default, and of a kind that makes the premises inaccessible or unfit for occupation or use. Hong Kong authority also indicates that the relevant “damage” does **not** extend to mere economic loss or loss of turnover; there must ordinarily be material physical damage affecting use of the premises. Minor defects or ordinary disrepair are usually insufficient. See *Vember Lord Ltd v The Swatch Group (Hong Kong) Ltd* [2022] 2 HKC 349, [2022] HKCFI 279 and *Wong Wai Ming v Sai Kung Flea Market & BBQ Paradise Co Ltd* [2009] HKCU 408.

**Application:**

- does it cover **water leakage** or more generally damage beyond the parties’ control;
- does it require the premises to be **unfit for occupation or use**;
- does it apply to **all rent** or only a fair proportion; and
- when does suspension begin and end.

If the lease has **no rent suspension clause**, the default position is unfavourable to you: rent usually remains payable, even if the premises become difficult or temporarily impossible to use as a restaurant.

If there is a clause, your position depends on severity. Hong Kong case law suggests that **serious** damage may qualify, but **minor leakage or discomfort** usually does not. A restaurant forced to close because of repeated leaks may have a better argument than a tenant complaining only of inconvenience, but it is still highly fact-sensitive.

**Conclusion:**

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Whether the shop’s contractual warranty term can limit you to repeated repairs only

**Issue:** Whether the shop can rely on its warranty wording to say that your only remedy is another repair.

**Rule:** Under Hong Kong sale of goods law, a seller’s contractual warranty is not the only possible source of rights. As a general Hong Kong principle, a manufacturer’s or retailer’s warranty is usually an additional promise about after-sales service; it does not automatically remove the buyer’s ordinary contractual rights if the goods supplied were defective or not of the quality required by the sale contract. Whether an “exclusive repair only” term is effective depends on the contract wording, any exclusion or limitation terms, and whether those terms are enforceable on the facts. Separately, where the buyer is limited to a claim for **breach of warranty**, the buyer may claim damages rather than reject the goods, and the measure of damages is the loss directly and naturally resulting from the breach; for quality defects, the prima facie measure is the difference between the value of the goods as delivered and the value they would have had if they complied with the warranty: Sale of Goods Ordinance (Cap. 26), s 55(1)–(3).

**Application:** On your facts, the laptop was sold as **new**, failed three times within four months, and two repairs have already not solved the problem. That materially weakens the shop’s position that another repair is necessarily your only remedy. A court would likely look beyond the label “warranty” and ask the more basic question: did the shop supply goods that complied with the contract? If the laptop was persistently defective, a term saying “repair only” may not fully answer that issue, especially if the repairs have already failed. The exact result would still depend on the written terms, what fault occurred each time, whether it is the same underlying defect, and whether the seller clearly and validly limited your remedies.

**Conclusion:** The shop is **not automatically entitled** to insist that you accept endless repairs merely because its warranty says so. Whether that term is effective depends on the contract and the defect history, but on the facts you gave, you appear to have an arguable case that your rights go beyond a third repair.

### 2. Whether, on these facts, you may have a right to a replacement, refund, or damages

**Issue:** Whether the repeated failures after two repairs may entitle you to a replacement, refund, or damages.

**Rule:** As a general Hong Kong principle, if goods supplied under a sale contract are defective, the buyer’s remedy depends on the seriousness of the breach and whether the buyer has already accepted the goods so that the matter is treated as a breach of warranty rather than a basis for rejection. If the defect is sufficiently serious and the buyer has not lost the right to reject, a refund may be arguable. If rejection is no longer available, damages remain available. Under Sale of Goods Ordinance (Cap. 26), s 55(1), where the matter is treated as a **breach of warranty**, the buyer may either use the breach to reduce or extinguish the price or sue for damages; but the buyer is not entitled, by reason only of that breach of warranty, to reject the goods.

**Application:** Your strongest factual points are:
- the laptop was sold as **new**;
- it has failed **three times in four months**; and
- **two repairs** have already been attempted without a lasting fix.

Those facts support an argument that the laptop is not of the standard reasonably expected of a new laptop and that the seller has already had a reasonable opportunity to repair but failed to provide a conforming product. That makes a **replacement** a commercially sensible remedy and may support a **refund** argument if the defect is fundamental or persistent.  

The main uncertainty is whether, after four months and two repairs, the law would treat you as having **accepted** the goods, in which case rejection/refund becomes harder and the claim may shift primarily to **damages**. Without the full contract, repair records, and technical evidence, it is not possible to say definitively whether a refund is already available as of right. But the repeated failures materially improve your position compared with a single isolated fault.

**Conclusion:** You likely have a credible basis to demand **more than another repair**. A **replacement** appears reasonably arguable on your facts. A **refund** may also be arguable, but it will depend on whether the defect is serious enough and whether your right to reject has been lost. ### 3. If the laptop is treated only as a breach of warranty rather than a right to reject the goods, what damages may be recoverable

**Issue:** If you cannot reject the laptop, what compensation can you claim?

**Rule:** For breach of warranty, the buyer may sue for damages: Sale of Goods Ordinance (Cap. 26), s 55(1)(b). The ordinary measure is the estimated loss flowing directly and naturally from the breach: s 55(2). For a quality defect, the starting point is the difference between the value of the goods when delivered and the value they would have had if they matched the contractual standard: s 55(3). If the buyer has already used the breach to reduce or extinguish the price, that does not prevent a further claim for additional loss not already compensated: s 55(4).

**Application:** In a laptop case, possible heads of loss may include:
- the reduction in value of the laptop because it was defective;
- repair-related loss not already cured by the seller;
- possibly reasonable consequential loss directly caused by the defect, if provable.

However, recovery for things like lost business, lost data, or downtime often turns on proof, causation, and whether the loss was within the scope of the seller’s responsibility. On the limited facts provided, the clearest claim is for the value loss inherent in receiving a repeatedly defective “new” laptop. If the seller offered repairs but the product remained unreliable, that strengthens a damages claim.

**Conclusion:** Even if a refund is disputed, you are not limited to accepting serial repairs forever. At minimum, a **damages claim** is likely arguable, and the statutory measure focuses on the loss caused by receiving defective goods.

If, however, the evidence shows only an ordinary dampness complaint or an unresolved but not fundamental defect, the landlord is more likely to say your departure was a **tenant repudiation**, in which case some further rent liability may arise.

### 2. Whether the landlord is entitled to keep the HK$48,000 deposit and also claim further rent after you moved out

**Issue:**  
Can the landlord both retain the deposit and ask for another two months’ rent until a new tenant is found?

**Rule:**  
A rental deposit is generally security for the tenant’s performance under the tenancy. Whether the landlord may forfeit it entirely, apply it against actual losses, or must account for the balance depends mainly on the proper construction of the tenancy agreement.

Where a tenant repudiates a tenancy and the landlord accepts that repudiation, the landlord may claim damages for the lost benefit of the term, but must take reasonable steps to **mitigate** loss by trying to re-let the property. The usual measure is lost rent for the relevant period, less rent received from a replacement tenancy and subject to reasonable mitigation. The **deposit should be brought into account** against the landlord’s recoverable loss: *Polyset Ltd v Panhandat Ltd* (2002) 5 HKCFAR 234. The landlord is not generally entitled to recover the same loss twice.

The landlord’s duty to mitigate is not especially onerous, but the landlord should act in the ordinary course of business to seek a replacement tenant. The burden of showing lack of mitigation generally falls on the tenant, though the factual picture matters.

**Application:**  
On the facts you gave, the landlord’s demand raises two separate questions:

1. **Can the deposit be retained?**  
   Possibly, but not automatically. The landlord cannot simply label the deposit “forfeited” unless the tenancy agreement supports that result or there is a valid claim against it. If the landlord’s actual provable loss is less than HK$48,000, the balance should ordinarily be returned. If you were legally justified in leaving, the landlord would have little or no basis to keep it except for any genuine outstanding sums or damage.

2. **Can another two months’ rent also be claimed?**  
   Possibly, but only if your departure was wrongful and the landlord actually suffered that loss after taking reasonable steps to re-let. Even then, the deposit must be credited against the loss. So if the claimed two months’ rent is the same loss period for which the deposit is being used, the landlord should not recover both without accounting.  
   
   For example, if your monthly rent was HK$24,000 and the landlord says the flat was vacant for two months, the gross claim may also be HK$48,000. In that situation, the deposit and the rent-loss claim appear numerically identical. The landlord would usually need to **set off the deposit against that claim**, not keep the deposit and then recover the same HK$48,000 again.

A further point in your favour is that the landlord says rent is due **until a new tenant moves in**. That wording implicitly recognises a mitigation issue. The landlord should be able to show reasonable efforts to re-let and when the premises were actually available and marketed.

**Conclusion:**  
The landlord is **not automatically entitled** to keep the deposit and separately recover another two months’ rent without giving credit for the deposit. If your move-out was justified, the landlord’s claim is materially weaker. If your move-out was wrongful, the landlord may still claim actual rent loss, but the deposit should ordinarily be applied against that loss rather than creating double recovery.

### 3. Whether you can recover the deposit, in whole or in part, and what your likely claim route is

**Issue:**  
Do you have a viable claim to recover the deposit, and where would such a claim usually be brought?

**Rule:**  
A tenancy deposit paid as security should be returned according to the tenancy agreement or, once its purpose is exhausted, at the end of the tenancy unless the landlord has a valid claim on it. Hong Kong authority recognises that the deposit is held subject to an obligation to return it when no valid claim remains: *Prudential Mall Ltd v Chou Kou Pim & Anor* [1990] 2 HKC 298 (CA); *Typhoon 8 Research Ltd v Seapower Resources International Ltd & Anor* [2002] 2 HKC 177, [2002] 2 HKLRD 660 (CA).

The landlord’s obligation to return the deposit is separate from the tenant’s obligation to pay rent and deliver up possession, though valid deductions may still be made where properly established. If the landlord refuses to return the deposit and the amount claimed is within the monetary jurisdiction of the Small Claims Tribunal, that is usually the first forum; higher amounts go to the District Court or Court of First Instance depending on quantum.

### 1. Whether you are likely to establish liability for a slip on an unmarked wet floor in a Hong Kong shopping centre

**Issue:**  
Whether the shopping centre is legally responsible for your injury where you say you slipped on an unmarked wet floor, but it says its cleaner inspected the area ten minutes earlier.

**Rule:**  
In a Hong Kong slip-and-fall claim, the claimant must prove, on the balance of probabilities, that there was a dangerous condition, that the defendant failed to take reasonable care, and that this caused the injury. The duty is one of **reasonable care**, not an absolute guarantee that every floor will always be dry or safe. The court will look closely at the evidence on how the accident happened, whether the floor was actually wet or hazardous, whether warnings were present, and whether there was a reasonable system of inspection and cleaning. These points are reflected in cases including *Pak Siu Hin v JV Fitness Li* 2019 HKCFI 2268, *Chan Wa Kun v Secretary for Ju* 2019 HKCFI 1688, *Chan Ying Wai v Secretary for Justice for and on behalf of Commissioner of Correctional Ser* 2021 HKCFI 221, and *Or Bik Yuk v Maxway Corporation Limited trading as Cheung Hing Foods* 2019 HKCFI 2783.

Those cases also show two practical points. First, the court will not infer negligence merely because a person fell. Second, if the surrounding evidence shows that the premises were left in a hazardous condition, liability can still be found.

**Application:**  
On your facts, the points helping your claim are:

A ten-minute inspection interval does **not automatically defeat** your claim. It is only one fact in the overall picture. The court would ask questions such as:

- Was the floor actually wet?
- How long had it been wet?

**Issue:**  
How is the estate likely to be shared where the deceased died intestate, was domiciled in Hong Kong, and is survived by a spouse and children.

**Rule:**  
For **movable property**, succession is generally governed by the law of the deceased’s **domicile at death**. For **immovable property**, succession is generally governed by the law of the place where the property is situated. On your facts, your father was domiciled in Hong Kong and the flat is in Hong Kong, so Hong Kong intestacy rules are likely to govern both the flat and the Hong Kong movable assets.

Where a person dies intestate leaving a **spouse and issue** (that is, descendants such as children), the surviving spouse is generally entitled first to the deceased’s **personal chattels** and a **statutory sum of HK$500,000** out of the residuary estate. The balance of the residuary estate is then usually divided so that **one half goes to the spouse** and **the other half is shared equally among the children**. The order of entitlement is governed by the **Intestates’ Estates Ordinance (Cap. 73), s 4**.

**Application:**  
On the facts you gave:

- your father died **without a will**;
- he was **domiciled in Hong Kong**;
- he left a **wife** and **three adult children**.

That means the usual Hong Kong intestacy position is likely to apply. Adult children are still “issue” for intestacy purposes; their age does not disqualify them.

So, subject to identifying what assets actually fall into the estate and subject to debts, funeral expenses, taxes, and administration costs:

- the **wife** is likely to receive:
  - any **personal chattels** that qualify as such;
  - **HK$500,000** from the residuary estate; and
  - **half of the remaining residuary estate**.
- the **other half of the remaining residuary estate** is likely to be divided **equally among the three children**.

If any asset passes outside the estate, it would not normally be included in this intestacy calculation.

**Conclusion:**  
The likely beneficiaries are the **surviving wife and the three children**. Broadly, the wife is likely to take **personal chattels + HK$500,000 + half of the balance**, and the three children are likely to share the **other half of the balance equally** under **Intestates’ Estates Ordinance (Cap. 73), s 4**.

### 2. Which assets are likely to form part of the estate, and which may fall outside it, including the Hong Kong flat, bank accounts, and Mandatory Provident Fund benefits.

**Issue:**  
Whether the flat, bank accounts, and MPF benefits are likely to be estate assets for intestacy purposes.

**Rule:**  
Assets standing in the deceased’s sole name are generally part of the estate unless there is a separate legal mechanism causing them to pass otherwise. Where a person dies intestate, the personal representatives hold estate property for administration. In an intestate estate, immovable property is generally held on trust for sale, and movable property is generally to be collected in, realised, and turned into money as needed for administration, subject to a power to postpone sale: **Probate and Administration Ordinance (Cap. 10), s 62(1)**.

As to the governing law point, succession to **immovable property** is generally governed by the law of the place where the property is located, while succession to **movable property** is generally governed by the law of the deceased’s domicile at death.

For **MPF benefits**, the position commonly depends on the particular scheme rules, trustee arrangements, any valid nomination, and whether the benefit is payable directly under the scheme rather than through the estate. That question is not determined solely by the intestacy rules.

**Application:**  
On your facts:

- **Hong Kong flat in sole name**: this is very likely an **estate asset**. Because it is immovable property in Hong Kong and was held in your father’s sole name, it would usually fall into the estate and be dealt with under Hong Kong succession law.
- **Bank accounts**: these are likely to be **estate assets** if they were in your father’s sole name. They would ordinarily be collected by the personal representatives as part of the estate administration.
- **MPF benefits**: these **may or may not fall into the estate**. In practice, MPF death benefits often require the trustee to decide payment in accordance with the scheme documents and any nomination or claim process. Depending on the scheme position, the money may be paid to personal representatives, or directly to dependants or other eligible recipients. So this item needs to be checked with the relevant MPF trustee and scheme documents before assuming it forms part of the estate.

Also, not every item connected with the deceased is necessarily distributable under intestacy. For example, liabilities, administration costs, and any non-estate arrangements affect the final amount available for distribution.

**Conclusion:**  
The **flat** and **sole-name bank accounts** are likely to be estate assets. **MPF benefits may fall outside the estate or may be paid into it depending on the scheme and trustee decision**, so that point needs specific verification before distribution assumptions are made.

   - death certificate;
   - family relationship evidence;
   - details of the flat, bank accounts, and other assets;
   - information on debts and funeral expenses; and
   - identity documents of the proposed administrator.

4. **Preserve and verify asset information immediately.**  
   Within the next few days, collect:
   - bank statements and account details;
   - title documents or Land Registry particulars for the flat;
   - MPF scheme and trustee details;
   - any insurance, shareholding, or pension documents; and
   - records of liabilities.

5. **Check the MPF position separately with the trustee.**  
   Ask the MPF trustee:
   - whether a death benefit nomination exists;
   - whether the benefit will be paid to the estate or otherwise; and
   - what claim documents are required.

6. **Do not distribute anything until the estate has been properly valued and administered.**  
   The estate must first meet debts, expenses, and administration costs. Only the net estate is distributed under intestacy.

7. **If the flat is to be sold later, plan for probate timing.**  
   Because the flat is likely an estate asset, any sale process normally has to wait until the administrator has formal authority.

**Risk assessment:**  
The main practical risks are:
- assuming that **MPF benefits** are part of the estate when they may not be;
- distributing money before confirming **debts and expenses**;
- delay or dispute over **who should apply** for the grant; and
- attempting to deal with the **flat** before formal authority is issued.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. 你在現有事實下，可能面對哪些與襲擊有關的刑事責任

**Issue:**  
你推對方一下，對方跌倒後下顎骨折；在香港法下，這可能構成甚麼襲擊類控罪。

**Rule:**  
香港法區分 **assault** 與 **battery**。就一般原則而言，對他人作出帶有敵意的身體接觸，可構成 **battery**；在日常語境中，警方或控方有時會把較廣義的「assault」用來涵蓋該類行為。普通襲擊可被檢控，而定罪後可判處監禁，法定最高刑罰為 1 年；這點見於 *Offences against the Person Ordinance (Cap. 212), s 40*。若襲擊造成 **actual bodily harm**，則可構成較嚴重的 **assault occasioning actual bodily harm**，其罰則見 *Offences against the Person Ordinance (Cap. 212), s 39*；法院資料亦顯示該罪最高可判監 3 年，並按個案具體事實評估嚴重性，見 *HKSAR v Liu Jinchang* 2017 HKCFI 2052。

**Application:**  
按你提供的事實，你承認自己曾「推了他一下」。單是這一下推撞，若屬蓄意而非意外，已足以令警方考慮普通襲擊／battery 類控罪。  
但由於結果是對方跌倒並出現 **下顎骨折**，警方更可能考慮較重的控罪，即 **assault occasioning actual bodily harm**。骨折通常屬明顯身體傷害，遠超輕微碰撞。即使你原本只是推一下，而非直接拳打腳踢，只要控方能證明你的推撞在法律上造成該傷勢，較重控罪仍有現實風險。

不過，是否能成立較重控罪，仍取決於若干事實，包括：  
- 你推撞的力度；  
- 對方當時是否正向你作出迫近或威嚇動作；  
- 跌倒是否因地面、酒精、第三者碰撞或其他介入因素造成；  
- 骨折是否直接由跌倒引起；  
- 有沒有 CCTV、目擊者、酒吧職員證供或醫療紀錄支持因果關係。

**Conclusion:**  
在你現時描述下，警方至少可能考慮 **普通襲擊**；若醫療證據確認下顎骨折與你推撞有直接關連，則較大風險是 **assault occasioning actual bodily harm**。實際控罪會高度取決於現場證據和傷勢因果關係。

### 2. 你是否可能提出自衛作為抗辯，及其成敗關鍵

**Issue:**  
若對方曾舉起玻璃樽向你走近，你推他一下是否可能屬合法自衛。

**Rule:**  
香港法一般承認，面對襲擊或迫切威脅的人，可使用 **合理所需** 的武力自衛；甚麼屬合理，屬事實問題，要看當時情況整體判斷。若所用武力明顯過度，則自衛抗辯可能失敗。法院亦指出，自衛不能建基於純粹猜測，必須有客觀證據把該議題帶出，例如閉路電視、目擊證人、現場痕跡或其他客觀材料；見 *HKSAR v Shek Ka* 2019 HKCFI 1323。

**Application:**  
對你較有利的事實是：  
- 對方不是空手，而是 **舉起玻璃樽**；  
- 對方並非靜止，而是 **向你走近**；  
- 你描述自己只是 **推了一下**，表面上屬單一、短暫、反應式行為，而非持續追打。

這些事實若有證據支持，確有可能構成自衛的基礎，因為玻璃樽可被視為具危險性的物件。法院在量刑案件中亦一再把玻璃樽、刀等物件視為可造成嚴重傷害的武器或危險工具；見 *HKSAR v Li Kwong-lam* 2018 HKCFI 1397、*HKSAR v Liu Jinchang* 2017 HKCFI 2052。這會有助你解釋：你當時為何認為需要立即隔開對方。

但自衛能否成立，關鍵不只在於你「感到害怕」，而在於客觀上是否可見有即時威脅，以及你所用力度是否相稱。對你不利的可能點包括：  
- 對方是否真的已作出揮樽、欲打你的動作，還是只是拿着樽走近；  
- 你是否本來可以後退、避開或由保安分隔；  
- 你推撞的力度是否超出阻止對方接近所需；  
- 對方跌倒是否與地面環境或醉酒失衡有關。  

如果 CCTV 顯示對方確實持樽逼近，而你只是即時推開以拉開距離，自衛抗辯會較有基礎。相反，若影像顯示對方未有作出即時攻擊，而你主動大力推撞，自衛便會較難成立。

**Conclusion:**  
你 **有可能** 主張自衛，但成敗主要取決於客觀證據是否顯示：對方當時構成即時威脅，而你只是用了 **合理所需** 的力度把他推開。若力度被認為過度，自衛抗辯可能失敗。

### 3. 如警方認為你造成對方下顎骨折，傷勢對控罪嚴重性和量刑的影響為何

**Issue:**  
對方下顎骨折這一結果，會如何影響案件嚴重性。

**Rule:**  
襲擊案件的嚴重性，通常受以下因素影響：實際受傷程度、所用物件或武力的危險性、造成更嚴重傷害的風險，以及整體案情。法院在涉及玻璃樽和刀具的案件中，均把造成較嚴重傷害風險視為重要加重因素；見 *HKSAR v Li Kwong-lam* 2018 HKCFI 1397 及 *HKSAR v Liu Jinchang* 2017 HKCFI 2052。對於 **assault occasioning actual bodily harm**，最高刑罰為 3 年監禁，見 *Offences against the Person Ordinance (Cap. 212), s 39*。

**Application:**  
下顎骨折通常會被視為相當嚴重的傷勢，較可能把案件推向較重控罪和較重處理。即使你沒有使用武器，若控方指你推撞導致對方重跌並骨折，傷勢本身已會明顯加重案件。  
另一方面，若醫療或現場證據顯示骨折與一些特殊因素有關，例如對方本身嚴重醉酒、踩空、向後失平衡、撞及路壆或其他硬物，這些都可能影響因果關係評估，從而影響控罪選擇或量刑。

**Conclusion:**  
**下顎骨折會顯著提高案件嚴重性。** 如控方能把骨折直接連結到你的推撞，案件很可能不止停留於普通襲擊，而會朝 **assault occasioning actual bodily harm** 的方向處理。
