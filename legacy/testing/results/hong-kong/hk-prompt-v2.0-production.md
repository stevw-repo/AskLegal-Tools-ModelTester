I reported the leaks on day ten because they appeared only when the containers were filled. Please analyse breach, the status of the email promise, the exclusion and limitation clauses, recoverable loss, and the immediate steps needed to preserve my position while I continue trading.

## Legal Issues Identified

2. Whether the sales director’s **email promise** that the first batch would arrive before the supermarket launch can be treated as a binding contractual obligation.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Breach: late delivery

**Issue:**  
Is the supplier liable for delivering the first batch three weeks late, even though the signed contract does not state the launch date?

**Rule:**  
A delivery date can be binding if it forms part of the contract, including by a pre-contractual assurance intended to be relied on. If not, the email may still be a representation, and in some cases a collateral promise. Whether it is legally operative depends on the full contractual setting: wording, timing, authority of the sender, reliance, and whether the written contract was intended to contain the whole bargain.

Where parties have signed a written contract that omits an earlier promise, the court will examine whether the earlier statement was intended to have contractual effect or was superseded by the written document.

**Application:**  
On the facts given, the email is helpful evidence for you, especially because:
- it came from the **sales director**;
- it concerned a commercially critical matter, namely your **supermarket launch**; and
- it appears you entered into or proceeded with the arrangement in reliance on it.

That said, the omission of the date from the signed contract creates a real difficulty. If the written contract appears comprehensive, the supplier will argue the email was no more than a sales assurance or estimate. Your position is stronger if:
- the supplier knew the launch date was essential;
- you communicated that the timing was critical;
- the email wording was firm rather than approximate; and
- there is no entire agreement or non-reliance wording.

**Conclusion:**  
You have an **arguable case** that the email promise formed part of the bargain or was a collateral promise, but this is **fact-sensitive** and cannot be treated as certain from the current facts alone.

### 2. Breach: leaking containers

**Issue:**  
Does delivery of 7,000 leaking containers amount to breach?

**Rule:**  
Under the **Sale of Goods Ordinance (Cap. 26)**, goods supplied in the course of business generally carry implied obligations as to **merchantable quality / fitness for purpose**, subject to the contract and any valid exclusion or limitation.

The decision in **Febest Europe Distribution OU v. Zhong Xing Da Technology Co, Limited 2019 HKCFI 2373** supports the basic position that defective goods which are not of merchantable quality can amount to breach, and that a buyer who properly rejects or claims for such defects may recover resulting loss subject to the contractual framework.

**Application:**  
Containers that leak when filled are very likely not fit for their ordinary commercial purpose. For a food-import business, that is a serious quality defect. On the facts stated, you have a strong prima facie case that the 7,000 leaking units breached the implied quality/fitness obligations and likely any express quality obligation if the containers were custom-made for your use.

The fact that the leakage became apparent only when the containers were filled is important. It supports your position that this was a **latent defect**, not something reasonably discoverable on ordinary initial inspection.

**Conclusion:**  
Yes. Subject to the contractual exclusions and notice provisions, the leaking containers are likely a **clear breach**.

### 3. Seven-day defect notice clause

**Issue:**  
Does reporting the defect on day ten defeat your claim?

**Rule:**  
A contractual notification requirement can be enforceable, including a clause that waives or extinguishes claims if notice is not given in time. In **CHIMBUSCO PAN NATION PETRO-CHEMICAL CO LTD v. ARKSTAR SHIP MANAGEMENT PTE LTD 2019 HKCFI 57**, the court upheld contractual machinery that barred a claim where specified notice requirements were not met.

However, where a party seeks to rely on an exclusion or limitation clause in Hong Kong, **reasonableness** may be critical under the **Control of Exemption Clauses Ordinance (Cap. 71)**. The same case indicates that the party relying on such a clause must properly raise and prove the factual basis for reasonableness.

**Application:**  
The supplier will say:
- the contract required notice within **seven days**;  
- you notified on **day ten**; therefore the claim is barred.

Your main responses are:

| Supplier argument | Your response |
|---|---|
| Notice was late on its face | The defect was **latent** and only emerged when the containers were filled |
| Seven days means seven days from delivery regardless | A strict reading may be commercially unreasonable if the defect could not reasonably be discovered within that period |
| Contract allocates inspection risk to buyer | For custom food containers, practical testing may require actual operational use, not mere visual inspection |

The strongest point in your favour is that the defect appeared only in use. A court may be more willing to scrutinise the clause’s reasonableness where the defect could not reasonably be detected within seven days.

**Conclusion:**  
The late notice point is a **serious risk**, but it is **not necessarily fatal**. Your best argument is that the clause should not bar claims for **latent defects discovered only on use**, or that its application here is unreasonable under **Control of Exemption Clauses Ordinance (Cap. 71)**.

### 4. Liability cap and exclusion of lost profits / customer claims

**Issue:**  
Are the limitation and exclusion clauses likely enforceable?

**Rule:**  
In business-to-business contracts, clauses limiting liability or excluding heads of loss may be effective, but their enforceability depends on construction and, where applicable, **reasonableness** under **Control of Exemption Clauses Ordinance (Cap. 71)**.

**CHIMBUSCO PAN NATION PETRO-CHEMICAL CO LTD v. ARKSTAR SHIP MANAGEMENT PTE LTD 2019 HKCFI 57** is relevant for two points:
1. notice/time-bar and exclusion structures can be effective; and
2. the party relying on them must establish reasonableness where that is required.

Relevant factual considerations usually include:
- relative bargaining strength;
- whether the clause was negotiated;
- whether the buyer knew or ought reasonably to have known of it;
- whether insurance was available;
- whether compliance with the clause was practicable; and
- whether the loss arose from a latent defect outside the buyer’s practical control.

**Application:**  

#### (a) Liability cap: price of the affected batch
This is potentially the most significant restriction. If valid and wide enough in wording, it may cap recovery for all claims arising from that batch, including defect-related direct loss.

Your challenge would be to argue the cap is unreasonable, especially if:
- the supplier knew the containers were for a time-critical supermarket launch;
- the goods were **custom** containers made specifically for your business use;
- the defect was latent; and
- the cap leaves you bearing losses far out of proportion to the supplier’s breach.

But commercial courts often uphold negotiated B2B caps unless the facts strongly show unreasonableness.

#### (b) Exclusion of lost profits and customer claims
This clause is directly aimed at losses such as:
- profits lost from the failed launch or missed sales; and
- claims or charge-backs imposed by your supermarket customer.

If valid, it is likely to catch the **HK$320,000 supermarket charge**, because that looks very much like a **customer claim** or customer-imposed liability. If the clause is clearly drafted, that item is at substantial risk of being excluded.

#### (c) Does the exclusion also affect late-delivery losses?
Possibly yes, depending on wording. If it excludes those heads of loss for any breach under the contract, it may apply to both late delivery and defective goods. If drafted more narrowly, it may apply only to quality claims.

**Conclusion:**  
The limitation and exclusion clauses are a **material obstacle** to recovery. They may well be enforceable unless you can show they are unreasonable or do not properly cover the specific loss claimed. The **HK$320,000** is particularly vulnerable to exclusion.

### 5. Recoverable loss

**Issue:**  
Which of your losses are most likely recoverable?

**Rule:**  
The general principle is that damages aim to put the injured party in the position it would have been in had the contract been performed. The buyer must also **mitigate**. **ZHAOQING KIDSTAR FOODS LTD v. UTILITY INDUSTRIAL LTD 2018 HKCFI 2668** supports recovery of the price difference and incidental costs reasonably incurred in obtaining substitute goods after breach, provided mitigation is reasonable.

**Application:**  

#### (a) HK$140,000 emergency replacements
This is your strongest damages item. It appears to be a direct and reasonable mitigation expense incurred to keep trading after defective or late performance.

On principle, that kind of replacement cost is usually recoverable, subject to:
- proof that it was reasonably incurred;
- proof of amount; and
- any effective contractual cap.

#### (b) HK$320,000 charged by supermarket for missed deliveries
This is weaker for two reasons:
1. it appears to be a **customer claim / customer charge**; and
2. the contract expressly excludes **customer claims**.

Even if foreseeable, that head is likely excluded if the clause is effective.

#### (c) Lost launch profits / reputational fallout
You mentioned missed deliveries and supermarket charges, but not a quantified lost-profit figure. In any event, the clause expressly excluding **lost profits** would likely bar this head if valid.

#### (d) Price reduction / replacement of defective units
If the 7,000 leaking units are established as defective, you may also have a claim measured by:
- the value of the defective goods,
- replacement cost, or
- other direct loss associated with the defective portion,  
again subject to the notice clause and liability cap.

**Conclusion:**  
Your **best recoverable item** is the **HK$140,000 emergency replacement cost**. The **HK$320,000 supermarket charge** is much more likely to be excluded. Any broader launch-related profit loss is also vulnerable to exclusion.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Possible claims against the seller

**Issue:**  
Can you claim against the seller for the statement that the largest customer had renewed for three years when in fact there was only an unsigned draft?

**Rule:**  
On the facts you gave, the most likely claims are:

- **Misrepresentation**: if a false statement of fact induced you to buy the company.  
- **Breach of warranty / disclosure obligations**: if the sale agreement contained warranties or disclosure provisions concerning key customers, contracts, revenue concentration, accuracy of information, accounts, or absence of material adverse change.  
- **Deceit / fraudulent misrepresentation**: if the seller knew the renewal was not actually in place, or was reckless as to whether it was true.

The dataset shows that in a share sale context, the court will focus closely on the contractual wording, the commercial purpose of disclosure obligations, and whether there was proper disclosure: **Invest Gain Limited v. Novel Good Li [2019] HKCFI 1633**. It also shows that a seller’s obligation to provide documents reasonably required for due diligence can be treated seriously, and failure to produce them may justify termination: **FONG KIN WA v. LI HAU YI 2023 HKCFI 765**.  

The dataset further indicates that due diligence and the buyer’s knowledge can affect a later claim, especially where the buyer actually knew the true position before completion: **Invest Gain Limited v. Novel Good Li [2019] HKCFI 1633**.

**Application:**  
Your facts suggest a potentially strong claim against the seller if you can prove:

- the seller represented, expressly or impliedly, that the customer **had renewed** for three years;
- that statement was **false** because only an **unsigned draft** existed;
- the point was **material** because it concerned the company’s largest customer and likely a substantial part of maintainable earnings;
- you **relied** on it in agreeing the HK$8 million price or proceeding to completion; and
- the loss flowed from the customer relationship not actually being secured.

Your case is stronger if the share purchase agreement, disclosure letter, management presentations, email exchanges, or due diligence responses used language such as:
- “renewed”;
- “contract secured”;
- “three-year renewal agreed”;
- “customer committed”; or
- statements in accounts or forecasts assuming that renewal as firm rather than contingent.

If the seller only provided an unsigned draft but described it as a completed renewal, that points toward **misrepresentation**. If the seller warranted that all material contracts were valid and in force, or that no major customer had indicated termination, that may also support **breach of warranty**.

If the seller knew there was no signed agreement, your strongest claim may be **fraudulent misrepresentation / deceit**, because exclusion clauses and contractual limitations are much less likely to protect deliberate dishonesty.

### 2. Effect of your own due diligence

**Issue:**  
Does the fact that you conducted your own due diligence prevent a claim against the seller?

**Rule:**  
Not necessarily. The dataset indicates that due diligence can affect a claim if the buyer had actual knowledge of the true position or if the relevant facts were properly disclosed: **Invest Gain Limited v. Novel Good Li [2019] HKCFI 1633**. But it also indicates that a seller cannot rely on the buyer’s failure to identify an obvious deficiency where the seller had a clear obligation to provide the correct documents or information: **Team Eight Group Limited v. Lo Yuk Yee [2021] HKCFI 1010**.

**Application:**  
The seller’s “you did your own due diligence” argument is **not a complete answer** unless the documentary record shows that:

- you were actually shown that the renewal was **unsigned only**;
- you understood there was **no binding renewal**; and
- you chose to proceed anyway on that basis.

If, instead:
- the seller or management positively stated the renewal was in place;
- the unsigned draft was buried in data-room material without being fairly explained; or
- the accountant’s report repeated the “renewed for three years” position,

then your own due diligence is less likely to defeat the claim. It may become an issue of **reliance**, **knowledge**, and possibly **contributory fault**, but it does not automatically absolve the seller.

### 3. Possible claims against the accountant

**Issue:**  
Can you sue the accounting firm for saying the largest customer had renewed for three years?

**Rule:**  
Potential causes of action are usually:

- **breach of contract**, if the retainer required the accountant to verify material information or report accurately; and/or
- **negligent misstatement / professional negligence**, if the accountant owed you a duty of care and failed to exercise reasonable professional skill and care.

On your facts, the key questions are:
1. **Scope of engagement** — was this a financial due diligence, quality of earnings review, vendor due diligence reliance report, or more limited compilation exercise?  
2. **What exactly did the report say?** Did it state the customer “had renewed”, or merely record management’s representation?  
3. **What disclaimers did it contain?**  
4. **Was it reasonable for the accountant to rely on management alone** without obtaining the signed renewal or qualifying the point?

**Application:**  
A claim against the accountant is potentially viable if:

- the report was addressed to you or expressly permitted your reliance;
- it represented the renewal as an established fact;
- that fact was sufficiently important that a competent accountant carrying out the agreed scope should have checked the supporting document or clearly qualified the statement; and
- you relied on the report in completing the acquisition.

Your case is weaker if the engagement and report clearly said:
- it was **not an audit**;
- the firm **did not verify management information**;
- it accepted management representations without independent confirmation; and
- you should not rely on it as confirmation of contractual status.

That said, even where a report contains such qualifications, the court will still look at the **actual wording**, the **commercial context**, and whether the accountant nevertheless conveyed a factual assurance. Contract interpretation and commercial purpose are treated objectively: **Invest Gain Limited v. Novel Good Li [2019] HKCFI 1633**.

### 4. The liability cap in the accountant’s retainer

**Issue:**  
Is a cap limiting the accountant’s liability to **twice the HK$180,000 fee** likely to be effective?

**Rule:**  
A liability cap in a professional retainer is not automatically invalid in Hong Kong, but its enforceability commonly depends on:

The dataset shows that limitation clauses are construed carefully and in their commercial context: **Invest Gain Limited v. Novel Good Li [2019] HKCFI 1633**.

The accountant will likely rely heavily on the cap. Whether it works will depend on matters such as:

### 5. Causation and loss

**Issue:**  
How do you show that the false renewal statement caused your loss?

**Rule:**  
You must show not only breach or misrepresentation, but that it **caused measurable loss**. The dataset expressly notes that causation is required for a breach of warranty claim: **Invest Gain Limited v. Novel Good Li [2019] HKCFI 1633**.

As to timing, the general rule is that damages are assessed at the date the cause of action arises, though the court can adopt another date if needed to avoid injustice: **LI LI HONG v. KILMOREY INTERNATIONAL HOLDINGS LIMITED [2020] HKCFI 372**.

**Application:**  
You therefore need to prove:

1. **Counterfactual position**  
   If you had known there was no signed renewal, would you:
   - not have bought at all;
   - postponed completion pending execution; or
   - paid substantially less?

2. **Value impact**  
   What was the company actually worth at completion without a secured three-year commitment from its largest customer?

3. **Link to subsequent departure**  
   The fact the customer left two months later is powerful evidence, but the seller/accountant may argue:
   - the customer might still have left even if there had been a signed renewal;
   - the loss was caused by post-completion service failures, integration issues, or market conditions;
   - the relationship was always terminable on notice; or
   - the company was worth HK$8 million anyway based on other earnings.

Your valuation evidence therefore needs to isolate the value attributable to the supposed renewal and show that the absence of a binding commitment was a real driver of overpayment.

### 1. Directors’ duties

**Issue:**

**Rule:**  
Under **Companies Ordinance (Cap. 622)**, directors owe statutory and fiduciary duties, including duties to act in good faith for the benefit of the company, to exercise powers for a proper purpose, and to avoid conflicts of interest. A director who causes the company to enter into a transaction benefiting connected persons on non-arm’s-length terms, or who uses company opportunities for personal benefit, may be in breach.

The dataset also supports the following points:

- Misappropriation of company assets or conduct amounting to breach of fiduciary duty can found an **unfair prejudice** claim: **XU LIU CHUN v. WU CHANG JIANG 2018 HKCFI 1575**.

**Conclusion:**

### 2. Unfair prejudice

**Issue:**  
Whether the conduct, viewed cumulatively, may justify an unfair prejudice petition.

**Rule:**  
Under **Companies Ordinance (Cap. 622)**, the court may grant relief where a company’s affairs are conducted in a manner **unfairly prejudicial** to the interests of members.

The dataset establishes these principles:

- “Unfairly prejudicial” is applied **flexibly** and context matters: **XU LIU CHUN v. WU CHANG JIANG 2018 HKCFI 1575**.
- The starting point is whether the parties have **departed from what they agreed among themselves**: **XU LIU CHUN v. WU CHANG JIANG 2018 HKCFI 1575**.
- **Prejudice** includes financial harm and other harm suffered in the person’s capacity as a member: **XU LIU CHUN v. WU CHANG JIANG 2018 HKCFI 1575**.
- Breach of a shareholders’ agreement may amount to unfair prejudice: **XU LIU CHUN v. WU CHANG JIANG 2018 HKCFI 1575**.
- Exclusion of a minority from management contrary to the founding understanding is a clear example of unfair prejudice: **XU LIU CHUN v. WU CHANG JIANG 2018 HKCFI 1575** and **CHIU WING KEUNG MIKEY v. INTERASIA CORPORATION LIMITED & ORS 2025 HKCFI 386**.
- Where there is exclusion from management in a small private company, a **buy-out order** is commonly the appropriate remedy: **CHIU WING KEUNG MIKEY v. INTERASIA CORPORATION LIMITED & ORS 2025 HKCFI 386**.
- The court looks at the matter **as a whole**: **CHIU WING KEUNG MIKEY v. INTERASIA CORPORATION LIMITED & ORS 2025 HKCFI 386**.

**Application:**  
Your unfair prejudice case appears potentially strong on the present facts because the allegations are **cumulative**:

| Related-party management fees | Possible extraction of value from the company in breach of the agreed approval mechanism |
| Diversion of a major customer | Possible transfer of business opportunity/value away from the company |
| Refusal to provide accounts | Restricts your ability to protect your economic interests and assess misconduct |
| Dilutive share issue | May reduce your stake below 10%, weakening your statutory position and bargaining power |
| Rejection of your equal investment offer | Supports an inference that the real aim is dilution rather than fundraising |

Particularly important points:

- The proposed share issue may be especially serious because dropping below **10%** could impair your practical leverage and some minority rights.
- The combination of **value extraction**, **business diversion**, **information denial**, and **dilution** is the type of pattern the court may view as oppressive or inequitable.

**Conclusion:**  
You appear to have a substantial basis to consider an **unfair prejudice petition**, with a realistic prospect of seeking both **interim relief** and a **final buy-out or unwinding-type relief**, depending on evidence.

I was a regional sales manager employed in Hong Kong by a medical-device company. **Response**

## Legal Issues Identified

1. Whether your employer was likely entitled to **summary dismissal** for emailing the customer spreadsheet to your personal account.
2. Whether the employer can lawfully **withhold HK$280,000 in commission**.
3. Your exposure to **confidentiality-based claims or an injunction**.
4. Whether the **12-month Asia-wide non-compete** is likely to be enforceable in Hong Kong.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Summary dismissal

**Issue:**  
Did emailing a customer spreadsheet to your personal email, without sending it to others, justify immediate dismissal without notice?

**Rule:**  
Under Hong Kong law, summary dismissal is reserved for serious misconduct, such as wilful disobedience, fraud, dishonesty, or conduct amounting to a repudiatory breach of the employment contract. The employer bears the burden of justifying it. The assessment is highly fact-sensitive and depends on the employee’s role, the nature of the information, the surrounding circumstances, and whether the conduct destroyed trust and confidence.

Section 9 of the **Employment Ordinance (Cap. 57)** provides:

> An employer may dismiss an employee without notice or payment in lieu of notice if the employee, in relation to his employment—  
> (a) wilfully disobeys a lawful and reasonable order;  
> (b) misconducts himself;  
> (c) is guilty of fraud or dishonesty; or  
> (d) is habitually neglectful in his duties.

The cases in the materials indicate:
- **Summary dismissal** requires serious misconduct or fundamental breach: **CHEUNG CHI WAH PATRICK v. HONG KONG CEMENT CO LTD 2017 HKCFI 1622**.
- Dishonesty is particularly serious, but must be proved to a high standard in substance: **HU YANGYONG v. ALBA ASIA LTD 2025 HKCFI 2484**.
- Conduct that genuinely undermines trust and confidence may justify dismissal even short of proven fraud, but the court still examines the actual facts closely: **HU YANGYONG v. ALBA ASIA LTD 2025 HKCFI 2484**.
- Employees owe duties of **fidelity and good faith**, but the scope depends on the facts: **LEE YIU HONG v. WELL-IN HOTEL SUPPLIES COMPANY LI 2020 HKCFI 2760**.

**Application:**  
On the facts you gave, the strongest point in your favour is that:
- you sent the spreadsheet **only to yourself**;
- you say the purpose was **to work at home**;
- there is no suggestion that you disclosed it externally, sold it, or used it for the competitor.

That does **not automatically make the conduct lawful**, because customer spreadsheets commonly contain confidential business information and possibly personal data. Sending them to a personal account can be a serious breach of policy or confidentiality obligations. For a **regional sales manager**, the employer may say you were senior enough to understand the risk.

However, immediate dismissal is not automatically justified merely because confidential material was emailed to a personal account. A Hong Kong court would likely ask:
- Was there a clear rule or policy prohibiting personal email use?
- Had working from home in this manner been tolerated?
- Was the spreadsheet highly sensitive?
- Did the employer discover this only after you accepted a competitor’s offer?
- Is there evidence of actual misuse, copying, forwarding, or solicitation?

Based on your limited facts, the employer may have a **credible disciplinary complaint**, but whether it reaches the high threshold for **summary dismissal** is less certain. If the evidence is only that you emailed it to yourself for home working and did nothing further, the employer may face difficulty proving misconduct serious enough to justify dismissal without notice.

**Conclusion:**  
Your employer’s case for **summary dismissal is arguable but not obviously strong** on the facts stated. Much will turn on the sensitivity of the spreadsheet, your seniority, and any internal IT/confidentiality policies.

### 2. Unpaid commission of HK$280,000

**Issue:**  
Can the employer withhold your accrued commission because of the dismissal and alleged misconduct?

**Rule:**  
If commission was already **earned under the contract**, dismissal does not automatically extinguish it. Employers sometimes try to rely on set-off or allegations of breach, but whether that works depends on the contractual terms and the nature of the alleged counterclaim.

The materials indicate:
- Employees are owed contractual remuneration unless there is a valid basis to deny it.
- In **LEE YIU HONG v. WELL-IN HOTEL SUPPLIES COMPANY LI 2020 HKCFI 2760**, the court dealt with unpaid commission and recognised that alleged breaches of fidelity/good faith do not automatically allow an employer to defeat a commission claim.
- The court also noted jurisdiction limits where an employer seeks to raise wider claims such as breach of confidence.

Under the **Employment Ordinance (Cap. 57)**, wages include certain contractual sums, and an employer cannot simply withhold earned sums without legal basis. Also, section 70 of the **Employment Ordinance (Cap. 57)** provides:

> Any term of a contract of employment which purports to extinguish or reduce any right, benefit or protection conferred upon the employee by this Ordinance shall be void.

**Application:**  
Key questions are:
- Was the commission already **earned** before dismissal?
- Does the contract say commission is payable only if you remain employed on the payment date?
- Is there a clawback or forfeiture clause tied to misconduct, resignation, or competition?
- Is the HK$280,000 purely commission, or partly discretionary bonus?

If the commission was contractually earned from completed sales before dismissal, your employer would usually need a **clear contractual right** to withhold it. Mere allegations of misconduct do not by themselves justify non-payment. If they say they have suffered loss and want to set it off, they would still need a proper legal basis and evidence.

If, however, the contract makes commission contingent on continued employment at payment date, or gives the employer a genuine discretion over payment, that may weaken your position.

**Conclusion:**  
If the HK$280,000 was **already earned commission under the contract**, the employer is **not likely entitled to withhold it simply because it accuses you of misconduct**. The precise contract wording is critical.

### 3. Confidentiality exposure and injunction risk

**Issue:**  
Are you exposed to an injunction for confidentiality breaches, even if you only emailed the spreadsheet to yourself?

**Rule:**  
Employees owe continuing duties regarding genuinely confidential information acquired during employment. Hong Kong courts may grant an injunction to restrain misuse or further disclosure of confidential information where there is a serious issue to be tried and damages may not be adequate.

The materials indicate:
- Employees owe a duty to maintain confidentiality of information acquired in employment: **GREATER CHINA APPRAISAL LTD v. TSANG KANG PO AND O 2018 HKCFI 2552**.
- The court may grant an **interlocutory injunction** to restrain breach of confidentiality.
- But a broader **springboard injunction** requires stronger evidence that confidential information has actually been misused to obtain an unfair competitive advantage: **GREATER CHINA APPRAISAL LTD v. TSANG KANG PO AND O 2018 HKCFI 2552**.
- Direct evidence of misuse matters. Mere suspicion is often insufficient for more extensive relief.

**Application:**  
Your risk is **real**, but it depends on what the employer can prove.

If the spreadsheet contains:
- customer identities,
- pricing history,
- margins,
- purchasing patterns,
- contact persons,
then the employer may plausibly argue it is confidential business information.

The fact that you sent it only to your personal account does not eliminate risk. The employer may seek orders requiring you to:
- stop using the spreadsheet,
- delete all copies,
- preserve devices for inspection,
- avoid contacting customers using that information.

However, based on the principles reflected in **GREATER CHINA APPRAISAL LTD v. TSANG KANG PO AND O 2018 HKCFI 2552**, a court is more likely to grant a **limited confidentiality injunction** than a broad springboard order unless there is evidence you have used or threaten to use the material for the competitor.

So the litigation risk is usually highest where:
- you joined or are about to join a direct competitor,
- the spreadsheet is current and commercially sensitive,
- there is evidence of customer solicitation,
- deletion has not been confirmed.

**Conclusion:**  
A **confidentiality injunction is a realistic risk**, especially to restrain use or disclosure of the spreadsheet. A broader injunction based only on speculation is less certain unless the employer can show actual or threatened misuse.

### 4. Enforceability of the 12-month Asia-wide non-compete

**Issue:**  
Is a 12-month restraint covering all of Asia likely to be enforceable?

**Rule:**  
Under Hong Kong law, post-termination restraints are enforceable only if they go **no further than reasonably necessary** to protect legitimate business interests, typically confidential information, customer connections, and workforce stability. A restraint aimed merely at preventing competition is unenforceable. Reasonableness is judged by factors including:
- your role and seniority,
- the geographical scope,
- the duration,
- the markets in which you actually worked,
- whether narrower protections would be adequate.

A confidentiality clause with **no time limit** may be enforceable so far as it protects information that remains genuinely confidential or amounts to a trade secret, but not as a blanket restraint over all information forever.

**Application:**  
A **12-month Asia-wide non-compete** is potentially vulnerable unless your role truly covered that territory and the employer can justify that breadth.

Relevant considerations:
- You were a **regional sales manager**, which may support some restraint more than if you were junior.
- But “**Asia-wide**” is broad. If your actual remit was only certain countries or key accounts, the clause may be wider than necessary.
- If the employer’s real concern is customer information and pricing, the court may regard **confidentiality and non-solicitation** as sufficient protection, making a broad non-compete harder to justify.
- A 12-month duration is not impossible, but it is on the heavier side and requires solid justification.
- The fact that the confidentiality obligations have **no time limit** does not automatically invalidate them, but the employer cannot use such wording to restrain ordinary know-how, memory, or general experience indefinitely.

In practice, the employer often has a better chance with:
- a confidentiality injunction,
- possibly a non-solicitation argument,
than with enforcing a very broad non-compete.

**Conclusion:**  
On the facts stated, a **12-month Asia-wide non-compete is not obviously enforceable and may be vulnerable for overbreadth**, especially if your actual customer base or territory was narrower. The **confidentiality obligations** are more likely to be enforceable, but only to protect genuinely confidential information.

2. Whether your company is likely to have only a **licence** or something stronger.
3. Whether licensing similar code and the same logo concept to a competitor is likely to infringe your rights.
4. What **urgent practical steps** are available before the competitor’s launch next week.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### Issue 1: Who likely owns the copyright?

**Rule**

Under the **Copyright Ordinance (Cap. 528)**, copyright subsists automatically in original works. Computer code is generally treated as a **literary work**, and a logo may qualify as an **artistic work**. The general position is that the **author is the first owner** of copyright. Where the creator is a **freelancer / independent contractor**, ownership depends on agreement; absent an effective assignment, the freelancer usually remains the copyright owner.

The material you provided points to the same distinction: for **commissioned works**, ownership depends on the parties’ agreement, and if there is **no agreement on ownership**, the independent creator generally owns the copyright. It also indicates that, under **section 15(2)**, a commissioning party may still have an **exclusive licence** to exploit the work for the purposes reasonably contemplated by the commission.

Any **assignment** of copyright must be **in writing and signed**: **Copyright Ordinance (Cap. 528)**.

**Application**

On your facts:

- the developer was a **freelancer**, not your employee;
- your emails describe **deliverables and price**;
- the emails **do not expressly assign intellectual property**.

On that basis, the developer is **more likely than not the legal owner of the copyright**, unless the email chain can properly be construed as a signed written assignment of copyright. From what you have said, that appears **unlikely**.

However, that does **not** mean you only bought a bare, revocable permission. There is a substantial argument that your company obtained at least an **exclusive licence for the reasonably contemplated purposes** of the engagement — namely, to operate your company’s booking platform and use the commissioned logo for your business.

So the likely position is:

| Work | Likely legal owner | Your likely rights |
|---|---|---|
| **Booking platform code** | Freelancer developer | At least a strong argument for an **exclusive licence** to use it for your business purposes |
| **Logo** | Freelancer developer | At least a strong argument for an **exclusive licence** to use the commissioned logo for your business purposes |

### Issue 2: Can the developer license similar code and the same logo concept to a competitor?

**Rule**

Copyright protects **expression**, not mere **ideas or concepts**. That principle appears in **Glowjoy Group Company Limited v. Wong Wai Chun 2019 HKCFI 2672**. The court also assesses infringement by comparing the works and asking whether the similarities are likely due to copying rather than coincidence. Whether a **substantial part** has been taken is judged by **quality rather than quantity**: **Glowjoy Group Company Limited v. Wong Wai Chun 2019 HKCFI 2672**.

Likewise, whether copying is established is a **question of fact**, with relevance given to access, objective similarity, and the nature of the similarities: **MORN CREATIONS LTD v. SWIRE RESOURCES LTD  2018 HKCFI 179**.

The material also indicates that if a commissioned work was created without an ownership agreement, the commissioning party may restrain objectionable exploitation falling outside what is consistent with its exclusive licence under **Copyright Ordinance (Cap. 528)**.

**Application**

#### (a) The code
If the competitor’s product uses the **same code** or a **substantial part** of your commissioned platform code, your position is materially stronger. Even if the developer retains ownership, you may argue:

- you have an **exclusive licence** for the intended business use;
- licensing substantially the same code to a direct competitor is inconsistent with that exclusivity;
- the competitor’s use may interfere with your licensed rights and may justify urgent injunctive relief.

If the code is only **similar in function**, that is weaker, because copyright does not protect the **idea** of a booking platform or its general functionality.

#### (b) The logo
Your wording is important. If the competitor is using only the **same logo concept**, that alone may be insufficient because copyright does not protect the underlying **idea**. But if the competitor’s logo reproduces the same visual expression, layout, shapes, arrangement, or other original features amounting to a **substantial part**, then you may have a stronger complaint.

So:

- **same concept only** → weaker copyright claim;
- **same or substantially similar visual execution** → stronger claim.

### Issue 3: What can you do before launch next week?

**Rule**

Where urgent relief is needed, the court can grant an **interlocutory injunction** if there is a serious question to be tried, damages are not an adequate remedy, and the balance of convenience favors relief: **Glowjoy Group Company Limited v. Wong Wai Chun 2019 HKCFI 2672**.

That case is particularly relevant because the court granted interim restraints in a copyright dispute where misuse of drawings and competing commercial use were alleged, and where damages were difficult to quantify.

Delay can count against interim relief, so speed matters: **AB Club Limited v. Chan Yin Ki 2020 HKCFI 2769 2020 HKCFI 2769**.

**Application**

Given the competitor is launching **next week**, your practical options are time-sensitive.

#### 1. Strong immediate letter before action
You can send an urgent solicitor’s letter to the developer and competitor asserting that:

- the booking platform code and logo were commissioned for your company;
- you have, at minimum, an **exclusive right to exploit them for the contemplated business purposes**;
- the proposed competitor launch would infringe or interfere with your rights;
- they must immediately provide undertakings not to launch, use, copy, disclose, sublicense, or deploy the code / logo pending resolution.

This is often the fastest commercial step.

#### 2. Demand preservation and disclosure
You can demand immediate preservation of:

- source code repositories;
- design files;
- version histories;
- email and messaging records;
- invoices / proposals with the competitor;
- launch materials and mock-ups.

That is useful both for negotiations and any injunction application.

#### 3. Apply urgently for an interlocutory injunction
If they refuse undertakings, an urgent court application is a realistic option, especially if:

- the competitor’s launch is imminent;
- your platform / branding is commercially sensitive;
- the harm from launch would be difficult to quantify;
- the evidence shows close copying of code or visual logo expression.

Your prospects are **better** if you can show actual copying of code or near-identical visual logo use, rather than only a similar business idea or branding concept.

#### 4. Focus the claim carefully
Your strongest points are likely to be:

- **exclusive licence / commissioned-work rights** under **Copyright Ordinance (Cap. 528)**;
- copyright infringement in any **substantial reproduction** of the code or logo;
- if relevant, possible **breach of confidence** for source code, specifications, workflows, or non-public business logic, consistent with the principles discussed in **Glowjoy Group Company Limited v. Wong Wai Chun 2019 HKCFI 2672**.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Containment and preservation

**Issue:** What should be done first, given a threat to publish customer data within 48 hours?

**Rule:** Under the **Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (Cap. 486)**, a data user must take all practicable steps to protect personal data against unauthorized or accidental access, processing, erasure, loss or use. The Ordinance also requires that personal data not be kept longer than necessary, but that does not prevent preserving material needed for investigation, legal claims, or regulatory response. Where personal data is disclosed online without consent with intent or recklessness as to specified harm, the anti-doxxing provisions may apply, and the Privacy Commissioner may issue cessation notices and pursue investigation.

**Application:** You already restored from backups, but that is only one part of containment. The immediate priority is to stop any ongoing unauthorized access, especially because the cloud vendor has identified use of a reused administrator password. The facts point to a likely compromise of privileged access. That means:
- disable or rotate all privileged credentials immediately;
- force password resets for all staff accounts, prioritising administrators and support personnel;
- enable and verify **multi-factor authentication** on all cloud, admin, VPN, email, identity, backup and hosting accounts;
- revoke active sessions, API keys, tokens and service credentials;
- isolate affected systems and preserve forensic images/logs before making avoidable changes;
- verify that backups are clean and segregated.

Because the attacker threatens publication, you should prepare for the possibility that exfiltration has already happened even if the full scope is not yet confirmed.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

**Issue:** Does this proposal create serious competition-law risk?

**Rule:** Under the **Competition Ordinance (Cap. 619)**, the **First Conduct Rule** prohibits agreements, concerted practices, and decisions of associations that prevent, restrict, or distort competition in Hong Kong. The concept of an agreement is broad and does **not** require a signed contract.  
The materials you describe involve two classic forms of **serious anti-competitive conduct**:

- **price-fixing**: competitors agreeing not to discount below an agreed level; and
- **market sharing / customer allocation**: competitors dividing major customers between themselves.

The rule can apply to conduct between competitors even if the arrangement is informal, oral, or only partly implemented. Exchange of future pricing intentions and customer allocation proposals is itself highly problematic.

The supplier’s support also increases risk. A supplier encouraging distributors to adhere to fixed or minimum resale prices may raise concerns about **resale price maintenance**, and if the supplier is helping competing distributors align their downstream prices or customers, that can aggravate the overall position.

The enforcement risk is particularly serious because **bid-rigging, market sharing, and price-fixing** have been treated by the Competition Tribunal as conduct harmful **by object**. In **Competition Commission v. Wing Hing Construction Company Limited & Ors  HKCT 3**, market sharing and price-fixing were found to contravene the First Conduct Rule. In **Competition Commission v. Nutanix Hong Kong Limited & Ors  HKCT 2**, bid-rigging was treated as serious anti-competitive conduct, for which no warning notice was required before proceedings.

**Application:** On your facts, the risk is **high**.

- “Stop discounting below an agreed price” is a proposed **price-fixing** arrangement.
- “Divide major customers between us” is proposed **market sharing**.
- The fact that “nothing has been signed” does **not** make it safe.

As for your **personal** position: the primary exposure is usually on the **company / undertaking**, but your involvement matters. If you participate in the discussions, send the email, or help implement the arrangement, you may become a key witness and expose yourself to internal, regulatory, and possibly court-related consequences. If your managing director is instructing you to take part, that does **not** remove the legal risk.

**Conclusion:** Yes. This creates a **serious competition-law risk** for the company, and potentially significant personal exposure for anyone who participates. The safest course is **not** to express interest, **not** to attend any meeting where pricing or customer allocation will be discussed, and **not** to engage in any further discussion of the proposal.

**Conclusion:** The exclusion clause may be a **serious obstacle** to recovering business losses, but it is **not automatically a complete defence** to every claim.

## hk-014

**Query**

I rented a Hong Kong flat for two years but moved out after ten months because persistent mould made my child's asthma worse. I repeatedly sent the landlord photographs and medical notes. The landlord says I broke the tenancy, is keeping my HK$48,000 deposit, and wants another two months' rent until a new tenant moves in. Am I liable, and how can I recover the deposit?

**Conversation ID**

`7eca7248-204a-4291-a7a9-53de06ed18c1`

**Response**

## Legal Issues Identified

1. **Whether your early move-out was legally justified** because of persistent mould and your child’s asthma.
2. **Whether the landlord can keep the HK$48,000 deposit**.
3. **Whether the landlord can also claim further rent** for the period before a new tenant is found.
4. **How you can recover the deposit** if the landlord’s deductions are disputed.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### Issue 1: Were you entitled to end the tenancy early?

**Rule:**  
In Hong Kong, this usually depends first on the **wording of the tenancy agreement**. A tenant is **not automatically entitled** to stop paying rent or end the tenancy simply because the landlord did not carry out repairs, unless the agreement gives that right or the facts are serious enough to amount to a legal repudiation or make the flat genuinely unfit for occupation.

The principles reflected in **Leung Wai Ling Isewesg v. Success Base Engineering Li 2020 HKCFI 202** are important:

- a landlord’s failure to deal with defects does **not automatically** mean the tenant can stop paying rent;
- a right of access to repair does **not necessarily mean** the landlord assumed a legal duty to repair;
- the **deposit and unpaid rent serve different functions**.

The materials also indicate that, unless the tenancy says otherwise, landlords are **not generally responsible for repairs or replacement items**, and a tenant must still pay rent despite disputes about repairs.

**Application:**  
On your facts, you do have potentially stronger evidence than a routine repair complaint because you say:

- the mould was **persistent**;
- you repeatedly notified the landlord;
- you sent **photographs and medical notes**;
- your child’s asthma was affected.

That said, on the information given, I cannot safely say that this automatically released you from the tenancy. The key points will be:

| Key point | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Repair clause** in the tenancy | If the landlord expressly agreed to repair structural/water ingress/mould problems, your position is stronger. |
| **Break clause** or early termination clause | If present, liability may be governed by that clause. |
| **Severity of the mould** | You would need evidence that the condition was serious, persistent, and materially affected habitability. |
| **Landlord’s response** | If the landlord ignored repeated notices, that may help your argument, but it does not automatically end liability. |
| **Whether you formally gave notice and returned vacant possession** | This affects when liability stopped and whether mesne profits / further rent may be claimed. |

**Conclusion:**  
Based on the principles above, **you are not automatically excused from liability simply because there was mould**, even if the landlord failed to act. Unless your tenancy agreement gave you a right to terminate, or the facts clearly establish a sufficiently serious breach, there is a **real risk** that the landlord is entitled to treat your departure as **wrongful early termination**.

---

### Issue 2: Can the landlord keep the deposit?

**Rule:**  
A rental deposit is generally treated as **security for the tenant’s performance** under the tenancy. The authorities in the materials show that:

- the landlord may be entitled to **forfeit or retain the deposit** depending on the tenancy wording;
- the deposit is **not automatically set off** against rent arrears for the tenant’s benefit;
- the deposit and a claim for rent/damages are **separate matters**.

This is consistent with **Leung Wai Ling Isewesg v. Success Base Engineering Li 2020 HKCFI 202**, where the court allowed the landlord to recover both:

- **forfeiture of the rental deposit**, and
- **arrears of rent**.

The materials also state that a tenant cannot simply decide to use the security deposit as rent.

**Application:**  
If your tenancy says the deposit may be forfeited or applied where the tenant ends the tenancy early, the landlord may have a contractual basis to keep it.

However, the landlord is **not entitled to arbitrary deductions**. If the landlord says the deposit is being kept because of:

- rent loss,
- damage,
- cleaning,
- reinstatement, or
- other breaches,

the landlord should be able to identify the contractual basis and justify the amount.

If the landlord is keeping the **entire HK$48,000** while also claiming further rent, that may still be legally possible **if the tenancy wording permits it** and if the landlord’s actual losses exceed the deposit.

**Conclusion:**  
On the facts given, the landlord **may be able to keep the deposit**, but that depends on the tenancy terms and whether the claimed losses are contractually and factually justified. The deposit is **not automatically recoverable** just because you left due to mould.

---

### Issue 3: Can the landlord claim another two months’ rent until a new tenant moves in?

**Rule:**  
If a tenant wrongfully terminates a tenancy, the landlord may claim damages for losses flowing from that breach, including **rent for the period until re-letting**, subject to the duty to **mitigate loss** by taking reasonable steps to find a new tenant.

The materials expressly state that a tenant may remain liable for **outstanding rent for the remaining term as consequential losses**, provided the landlord took reasonable steps to mitigate. That approach is also reflected in **THE HONG KONG HOUSING AUTHORITY v. SUPER HAPPY INVESTMENT LI 2018 HKCFI 2674**, where the court accepted that a landlord can recover loss of rental income after wrongful repudiation, subject to mitigation.

**Application:**  
If your departure is treated as a breach, the landlord may be entitled to claim:

- rent for the period reasonably needed to re-let the flat; or
- the shortfall up to re-letting,

**provided the landlord acted reasonably** to mitigate loss.

So the landlord’s claim for **another two months’ rent** is **not inherently unlawful**. The real questions are:

1. **Was your departure a wrongful termination?**
2. **Did the landlord act reasonably to re-let the flat promptly?**
3. **Was two months a reasonable period in the market?**
4. **Did the landlord leave the flat vacant unnecessarily?**

If the landlord made little effort to re-let, their claim may be reduced.

**Conclusion:**  
Yes, **the landlord can potentially claim further rent or damages until a new tenant moves in**, but only if your departure was legally a breach and the landlord can show reasonable mitigation.

---

### Issue 4: How can you recover the deposit?

**Rule:**  
Where there is a dispute over deposit deductions, the tenant may bring a civil claim to recover the deposit. The forum depends on the amount.

The materials state:

- **Small Claims Tribunal**: claims for **HK$75,000 or less**
- **District Court**: claims exceeding **HK$75,000** but not exceeding **HK$3,000,000**
- **Court of First Instance**: above **HK$3,000,000**

See also **Small Claims Tribunal Ordinance (Cap 338), s29(1)(c)**.  
Official legislation site: <https://www.elegislation.gov.hk/>

**Application:**  
Your deposit is **HK$48,000**, so a claim for the deposit alone would generally fall within the **Small Claims Tribunal**.

But there is an important practical point: if the landlord counterclaims for rent and other losses, jurisdiction may become more complicated depending on the total sums claimed and how the dispute is framed.

To recover the deposit, you would usually need evidence showing:

- the mould condition and duration;
- your complaints to the landlord;
- the landlord’s failure or refusal to address it;
- medical evidence linking the condition to your child’s asthma;
- the tenancy agreement;
- proof of handover / return of keys;
- any evidence that the landlord re-let quickly or failed to mitigate;
- any messages showing what the landlord says the deposit is being used for.

**Conclusion:**  
You can **seek recovery of the deposit through the Small Claims Tribunal** if the dispute remains within its monetary limit. Your prospects will depend heavily on the tenancy wording and your evidence that the landlord was in serious breach or that the deductions are unjustified.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

**Issue 1: Can your compensation be calculated exactly now?**

**Rule:** In Hong Kong personal injury cases, damages depend heavily on medical evidence, prognosis, causation, credibility, time off work, residual disability, and whether future treatment is reasonably necessary. In slip-and-fall cases and personal injury assessments, the court looks at the actual evidence rather than making assumptions. Quantum assessment is highly fact-specific.

**Application:** On your facts, important missing matters include:
- whether the fracture required surgery already;
- whether the nerve pain is temporary or permanent;
- whether doctors support **further surgery** as probable;
- whether you have returned to full duties;
- whether there is any future loss of earning capacity;
- medical, travelling, rehabilitation and care expenses;
- whether there is CCTV, incident reporting, or cleaning logs;
- whether the wet floor arose very shortly before the accident.

Your **past wage loss** can be estimated from your figures only:
- HK$55,000 per month × 4 months = **HK$220,000**

But that is only one possible head of damage. It does **not** allow an exact overall compensation figure. Pain and suffering, future loss, future treatment costs, and any loss of earning capacity cannot responsibly be fixed without medical and factual evidence.

**Conclusion:** I cannot responsibly calculate **exactly how much** you should receive on the present facts. **Quantum in personal injury cases is highly technical, and you should consult a lawyer.**

---

**Issue 2: Can you claim against the owner, manager, or cleaning contractor?**

**Rule:** An occupier of premises owes lawful visitors a duty to take reasonable care for their safety. The injured person must still prove negligence and causation. The law does not require a floor to be perfectly dry at all times, but it does require a **reasonable cleaning and inspection system** and reasonable precautions such as warning signs where appropriate. The burden remains on the claimant to show the accident was caused by the defendant’s negligence.

In **OR BIK YUK v. MAXWAY CORPORATION LIMITED trading as CHEUNG HING FOODS 2019 HKCFI 2783**, the court held the defendant liable where a hazardous walking surface outside the premises caused a slip. In **Chan Wa Kun v. Secretary for Justice 2019 HKCFI 1688**, the claim failed because the plaintiff did not prove that the fall was caused by the alleged dangerous condition rather than some other cause. The general position reflected in slip-and-fall claims is also consistent with **Occupiers Liability Ordinance (Cap. 314)**.

**Application:** On your facts:
- The **shopping centre owner** may be liable if it was the occupier or retained sufficient control over the common area.
- The **manager** may be liable if it managed and controlled the area and was responsible for safety, cleaning systems, and warning procedures.
- The **cleaning contractor** may be liable if its staff negligently failed to clean, inspect, cordon off, or display warning signs.

In practice, more than one party may be sued if responsibility is unclear at the outset. Liability usually turns on **control of the accident location** and **who was responsible for the cleaning system there**.

A claim against only one party may be risky if another party actually controlled the area. So from a liability-analysis perspective, the potentially proper defendants are often:
- the **occupier** of the common area;
- the **property manager** if operational control was delegated to it;
- the **cleaning contractor** if its negligent acts or omissions caused the danger.

**Conclusion:** Yes, you may potentially have claims against **one or more** of the owner, manager, and cleaning contractor. The key question is **who had control of the wet-floor area and who was responsible for inspection, cleaning, and warning signage at the time**.

---

**Issue 3: Does the alleged inspection 10 minutes earlier defeat your claim?**

**Rule:** A defendant is not automatically protected merely because it says there was an earlier inspection. The court examines whether the inspection system was **reasonable in the circumstances**, whether it was actually carried out, whether records are reliable, how busy the area was, how foreseeable spillages were, and whether warnings should have been in place.

**Application:** A recorded inspection **10 minutes earlier** may help the shopping centre, but it is **not conclusive**. Relevant points would include:
- Was the floor actually wet at the time?
- Was there **no warning sign**?
- Was this a high-footfall area where more frequent monitoring was needed?
- Was the substance obvious and present long enough that reasonable staff should have dealt with it?
- Are there CCTV footage, incident records, staff rosters, and cleaning logs?
- Did the cleaner truly inspect the precise spot, or only pass nearby?

If the wet floor was present long enough, or if the cleaning/inspection system was inadequate for that location, the 10-minute point may not defeat the claim. But if the spill happened moments before your fall and the system was otherwise reasonable, liability becomes harder to prove.

**Conclusion:** The “10 minutes earlier” point is a **defence point**, not a complete answer. It may reduce or defeat liability only if the overall cleaning and warning system was reasonable and the hazard arose too recently to be addressed.

### 4. 子女贍養費

- 基本生活費；

- 醫療保險或特別醫療；  
- 住屋中與子女相關的合理部分。  

即使子女不與你同住，若你的收入明顯較低，也可能仍需按實際照顧比例分攤部分支出，但整體上高收入一方通常負擔較重。

**Conclusion:**  
子女贍養費不會只看誰收入高，而是看**子女合理需要 + 雙方支付能力**。在你所述情況下，對方大概率需承擔較大份額。

**Query**

My father died domiciled in Hong Kong without a will. He left a wife, three adult children, a Hong Kong flat held in his sole name, Mandatory Provident Fund benefits, and several bank accounts. Who is likely to inherit, which assets may fall outside the estate, and what must I do before dealing with the flat or money?

**Response**

## Legal Issues Identified

1. **Who is likely to inherit** where your father died **intestate** in Hong Kong.
2. **Which assets may fall outside the estate**.
3. **What must be done before anyone can deal with the flat or bank money**.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

**Issue:** Who is likely to inherit?

**Rule:** If a person dies **without a will**, the estate is distributed under the intestacy rules. The right to apply for a **Grant of Letters of Administration** is governed by the priority order in **rule 21 of the Non-Contentious Probate Rules (Cap. 10A)**. A **Grant of Representation** is required to deal with the deceased’s Hong Kong assets.

**Application:** On the facts you gave, your father left:
- a **surviving wife**; and
- **three adult children**.

That means the persons likely to benefit under intestacy are **the widow and the children**, not just one family member alone. The spouse is the primary claimant together with the children under the intestacy regime, and the children would share the children’s portion **equally** between them.

As for who may apply to administer the estate, the **surviving spouse** has first priority to apply for **Letters of Administration**, followed by the children if needed.

**Conclusion:** The likely beneficiaries are **your father’s wife and his three adult children**. The surviving spouse is ordinarily the person with first priority to apply for the grant.

---

**Issue:** Which assets may fall outside the estate?

**Rule:** Not all assets owned or connected with the deceased necessarily form part of the estate. The following commonly **do not** form part of the estate:
- **Property held in joint tenancy**, which passes by survivorship;
- **Nominated property**, such as some policy or pension-type benefits payable directly to another person;
- **Life insurance policies written in trust** for a named beneficiary.

The dataset also indicates that for **Mandatory Provident Fund** benefits, one should check the relevant MPF/trustee arrangements and scheme documentation.

**Application:** On your facts:
- The **Hong Kong flat held in his sole name** is likely part of the estate.
- The **bank accounts** are likely part of the estate, unless any account was held jointly and passed by survivorship.
- The **MPF benefits** may require separate treatment and may not necessarily be dealt with in the same way as ordinary estate assets. Whether they fall into the estate can depend on the relevant scheme and payment arrangements.

**Conclusion:**  
Likely **inside the estate**:
- the flat in sole name;
- bank accounts in sole name.

Potentially **outside the estate** or subject to separate treatment:
- any **jointly held assets**;
- assets with a valid **nomination**;
- possibly **MPF benefits**, depending on the scheme/trustee arrangements.

---

**Issue:** What must be done before dealing with the flat or the money?

**Rule:** A **Grant of Representation** is required to deal with the deceased’s assets in Hong Kong. Where there is **no will**, the appropriate grant is **Letters of Administration**. An administrator’s power comes from the grant itself, not from the date of death.

Before distribution, the personal representative must:
- identify and collect the estate assets;
- ascertain and pay debts, funeral expenses, and administration expenses;
- take reasonable steps to check for outstanding debts;
- avoid distributing too early.

The materials also indicate:
- the grant should be registered at the **Land Registry** for real property;
- the grant is normally produced to the **banks** to deal with bank accounts;
- a prudent course is to advertise for creditors under **section 29 of the Trustee Ordinance (Cap. 29)** and allow at least 2 months for claims;
- full distribution is commonly delayed until the end of the **executor’s year**.

**Application:** So, before anyone sells, transfers, withdraws, or distributes the flat or money:
1. Someone entitled should apply for **Letters of Administration**.
2. Once issued, the administrator can use the grant to deal with the **bank accounts** and register the authority concerning the **flat**.
3. The administrator must first deal with **debts, funeral expenses, and estate expenses** before distributing anything.
4. Care should be taken not to assume that **MPF** can be withdrawn in the same manner as estate money.

**Conclusion:** You should **not deal with the flat or withdraw/distribute the money first**. The estate should first be administered by a properly appointed **administrator** under a **Grant of Letters of Administration**.

## Recommended Action Plan

**Immediate Steps**
1. Confirm whether your father truly left **no will**.
2. Identify who will apply for **Letters of Administration** — usually the **surviving spouse** first.
3. Gather asset information:
   - title details of the flat;
   - bank statements/account details;
   - MPF scheme/trustee details.
4. Check whether any asset was held **jointly** or subject to a **nomination**.

**Medium-Term Strategy**
1. Apply for the **Grant of Letters of Administration**.
2. After the grant is issued:
   - present it to the **banks**;
   - deal with the **flat** through the Land Registry process;
   - contact the **MPF trustee** for its claim procedure.
3. Ascertain and settle all debts and expenses before any distribution.

**Risk Assessment**
- If anyone deals with estate assets **without a grant**, that can create practical and legal difficulties.
- If the estate is distributed before debts are settled, the **administrator may be personally liable**.
- If any asset appears to be joint property or nominated property, treating it as estate property by mistake can cause disputes.

**Response**

## Legal Issues Identified

1. 你在酒吧外推跌他人，對方下顎骨折，**可能涉及甚麼刑事罪行**
2. 在你所述情況下，**自衛**是否可能成為抗辯理由
3. 警方要求你明天到警署「協助調查」，但未說明你是證人還是疑犯；你在**錄口供**時有甚麼權利及風險
4. 警方如要求你**交出或搜查手機**，你應注意甚麼

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. 你可能面對的刑事責任

**Issue：** 推跌對方並造成下顎骨折，可能構成甚麼罪行？

**Rule：**  
在香港，若有人襲擊他人並造成身體傷害，常見罪名包括：

- **普通襲擊**
- **襲擊致造成身體傷害**，可按 **Offences against the Person Ordinance (Cap. 212) section 39** 處理

根據 **HKSAR v. LIU Jinchang 2017 HKCFI 2052**，襲擊致造成身體傷害的最高刑罰可達 **3年監禁**。該案亦反映，量刑會視乎個案事實，包括：
- 使用物件作武器與否
- 傷勢嚴重程度
- 是否有預謀
- 是否認罪
- 有無刑事紀錄

根據 **HKSAR v. LI Kwong-lam 2018 HKCFI 1397**，法庭會特別重視：
- 是否使用玻璃樽等危險物件
- 攻擊有否造成更嚴重傷害的風險
- 被告過往暴力紀錄
- 醉酒一般**不屬減刑理由**

**Application：**  
你目前描述是：對方先舉起玻璃樽並向你走近，你則推了對方一下，對方跌倒後下顎骨折。

就警方及控方角度，**下顎骨折已屬相當明顯的身體傷害**，因此你最現實可能面對的，至少是 **襲擊致造成身體傷害** 的調查甚至檢控，而不一定只屬普通襲擊。

但實際會否起訴、起訴甚麼罪名，仍取決於警方掌握的證據，例如：
- 閉路電視是否拍到對方先作出威嚇
- 對方當時與你的距離
- 玻璃樽是否真的被舉起作攻擊姿態
- 你只是單次推開，還是有後續追打
- 當時你是否可安全後退
- 有無證人支持你的版本

**Conclusion：**  
按你現時所述，**最可能被考慮的罪名是襲擊致造成身體傷害**。但若證據支持你只是為了即時保護自己而作出合理反應，則你可能有**自衛抗辯**。

### 2. 你可否主張自衛

**Issue：** 對方舉起玻璃樽向你走近時，你推他一下，是否可能屬合法自衛？

**Rule：**  
香港刑法承認**自衛**。核心問題通常是：

1. 你當時是否**真誠相信**自己面對即時威脅  
2. 你所用武力是否在當時情況下**合理及相稱**

法律一般不要求人在突發衝突中作出完美判斷，但若武力明顯超出需要，便可能失去自衛抗辯。

**Application：**  
對你有利的因素包括：
- 對方據稱**舉起玻璃樽**
- 對方**向你走近**
- 玻璃樽本身可被視為危險物件
- 你所說只是**推了一下**，而非持續攻擊

對你不利的因素可能包括：
- 若閉路電視顯示對方未真正作勢打你
- 若你當時其實可以離開而無需動手
- 若警方認為你推的力度過大，或帶有報復性
- 若事後證據顯示你先挑起衝突

**Conclusion：**  
**自衛在你這類案情中是有現實可能性的抗辯。**  
但能否成立，關鍵不在於結果是否嚴重，而在於**你推人的當刻是否面對即時威脅，以及推那一下是否合理**。下顎骨折雖然令案件嚴重化，但**傷勢嚴重本身不會自動否定自衛**。

---

### 3. 明天到警署錄口供前，你應注意甚麼

**Issue：** 警方只說你去「協助調查」，你應如何應對？

**Rule：**  
根據警方調查的一般原則：

- 如你只是被要求**自願協助調查**，你**沒有一般法律責任**必須到警署或留在警署
- 如你其後被**拘捕**，警方須告知你被懷疑干犯的罪行及拘捕理由
- 你有**緘默權**
- 在錄取口供前，警方應作出警誡
- 你可先尋求法律意見，再決定是否回答問題
- 你可拒絕參與錄影會面
- 完成會面後，你應獲提供口供紀錄副本

資料亦指出，如你只是按警方要求協助調查，原則上你可自行決定是否自願協助，並可在自願性質下離開。

**Application：**  
你現在最重要的是先分清楚自己的身分：

| 情況 | 法律位置 | 風險 |
|---|---|---|
| 只是證人 | 可自願協助 | 說錯話後，日後可能反過來成為對你不利證據 |
| 警方已視你為疑犯但未明說 | 風險較高 | 你一旦詳細解釋事件，可能被用作檢控基礎 |
| 已被正式拘捕 | 有警誡及拘留權利保障 | 你的口供及手機內容都可能成為證據 |

你到警署前，至少應先問清楚：
- 你是以**證人**還是**疑犯**身分協助調查
- 今次是否**自願錄口供**
- 你是否**可隨時離開**
- 警方是否打算**拘捕你**

若警方開始就衝突細節盤問你，而你又未清楚自己身分，風險會上升。因為你本來想解釋「自衛」，但表達稍有偏差，可能變成承認你有推人、先動手、或當時並非真正受威脅。

**Conclusion：**  
在未清楚你是證人還是疑犯前，**不要急於作詳細書面或錄影口供**。你有權保持沉默，並先了解自己的法律處境。

### 4. 錄口供時的實際注意點

**Issue：** 若警方要你錄口供，你最應注意甚麼？

**Rule：**  
資料顯示：
- 你有**緘默權**
- 可先要求**法律意見**
- 可拒絕參與**錄影會面**
- 若你選擇作供，警方應先作警誡
- 口供完成後，你有權閱讀及要求修改錯漏，再簽署
- 若你事後發現口供有錯，可盡快要求更正或補充

**Application：**  
對你而言，最重要的是避免以下風險：
- 在緊張下作出猜測或誇大，例如「我以為他一定會打我」
- 把不確定事情說成確定事實
- 說出帶情緒字眼，如「我忍無可忍所以推他」
- 為了解釋自己而補充太多細節，反令警方認為你有動機或蓄意

若你決定回答，應只陳述你**親眼所見、親身所知**的事實。  
例如你可區分：
- 你**看見**他舉起玻璃樽
- 你**感到**他將要襲擊你
- 你**做了**一下推開動作
- 你**沒有**繼續攻擊

**Conclusion：**  
錄口供時最重要不是「盡量解釋」，而是**避免說錯、說多、或說出不必要的推測**。

### 5. 手機：警方要求交出或查看時，你應注意甚麼

**Issue：** 警方可否查看你的手機？你是否必須解鎖？

**Rule：**  
資料指出：

- 警方要搜查手機內的**數碼內容**，一般需要**手令**
- 如不切實可行先取手令，而又有合理基礎認為需要即時搜查以作調查或保障安全，才可能作**無手令搜查**
- 無手令搜查的範圍應限於與調查目的有關的內容
- 警方應盡快就搜查目的及範圍作書面記錄
- **被捕人沒有法律責任解鎖受密碼保護的手機**

另外，警方在合法拘捕後，對你身上的物品及與調查有關物件可有較廣泛處理權力；若涉及正式搜證，也可能透過手令處理。

**Application：**  
在你的情況，手機可能被警方視為有調查價值，例如：
- 酒吧外事發前後的通訊紀錄
- WhatsApp/Signal 對話
- 現場片段
- 定位紀錄
- 與證人聯絡內容

但重點是分清兩件事：

| 問題 | 一般原則 |
|---|---|
| 警方可否要求你交出手機實物 | 視乎是否拘捕、是否有手令、或是否屬與調查有關物件 |
| 警方可否查看手機內資料 | 一般需手令；緊急例外情況除外 |
| 你是否必須提供密碼或解鎖 | 資料所示，**沒有法律責任**解鎖受密碼保護手機 |

**Conclusion：**  
若警方要求查看你手機，**你應先問清楚法律基礎**：是你自願同意、還是根據手令、還是因緊急情況作無手令搜查。就現有資料，**你沒有法律責任替警方解鎖密碼保護手機**。
