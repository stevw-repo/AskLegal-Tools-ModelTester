For the first test, **“usually”** does not mean invariably or even more often than not; it carries the sense of **habitual**, **customary** or **regular** work: *Hanns v Greyhound Pioneer Australia Ltd* (2006) 196 FLR 361; [2006] ACTSC 5, [22], [27]. A worker may usually work in more than one jurisdiction, and the inquiry looks to where the work actually takes place, together with work history, intended future arrangements, and periods working elsewhere, excluding temporary arrangements outside the ACT for up to six months: **Workers Compensation Act 1951 (ACT) s 36B(6)**; *Avon Products Pty Ltd v Magrit Falls* [2010] ACTCA 21, [28], [30].

If no single answer emerges, the second test asks where the worker is **usually based** for the employment, considering matters such as any contractual work location, where the worker routinely attends to receive directions or collect equipment, where the worker reports, and from where wages are paid: **Workers Compensation Act 1951 (ACT) s 36B(3)(b)**; *Avon Products Pty Ltd v Falls* (2009) 236 FLR 212; [2009] ACTSC 141, [53].

If that still produces no single answer, the third test looks to the employer’s **principal place of business in Australia**: **Workers Compensation Act 1951 (ACT) s 36B(3)(c)**; *Avon Products Pty Ltd v Falls* (2009) 236 FLR 212; [2009] ACTSC 141, [55].

**Response**

## Legal Issues Identified

1. Whether, under **Australian constitutional law**, the Victorian prohibition is invalid to the extent it is inconsistent with the Commonwealth law that authorises your licensed service.
2. Whether a **Commonwealth licence or statutory authorisation** amounts to a mere **permission** or instead confers a **right, privilege or immunity** such that a Victorian law prohibiting part of the service would alter, impair or detract from Commonwealth law.
3. Whether there is **direct inconsistency** because it is impossible to obey both laws, or because the Victorian law takes away a right, privilege or immunity conferred by Commonwealth law.
4. Whether there is **indirect inconsistency** because the Commonwealth law was intended to regulate the relevant subject matter exclusively, completely or exhaustively, leaving no room for Victorian regulation of that part of the service.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Issue
Whether, under **Australian constitutional law**, the Victorian prohibition is invalid to the extent it is inconsistent with the Commonwealth law that authorises your licensed service.

**Rule:** Under **s 109 of the Australian Constitution**, when a State law is inconsistent with a Commonwealth law, the State law is invalid **to the extent of the inconsistency**. In this context, “invalid” means **inoperative**, so the State law is displaced only so far as the inconsistency exists and may revive if the inconsistency later disappears: Australian Constitution s 109; *Butler v A-G (Vic)* (1961) 106 CLR 268, 283; *Wenn v A-G (Vic)* (1948) 77 CLR 84, 122. Inconsistency may arise in at least two main ways:

- **Direct inconsistency**: where the laws directly conflict, collide textually, make contradictory provision on the same topic, or one takes away a right, privilege or immunity conferred by the other: *University of Wollongong v Metwally* (1984) 158 CLR 447, 455–6, 483–4; *Miller v Miller* (1978) 141 CLR 269, 275; *R v Credit Tribunal; Ex parte General Motors Acceptance Corp, Australia* (1977) 137 CLR 545, 563; *Clyde Engineering Co Ltd v Cowburn* (1926) 37 CLR 466, 490; *Dickson v R* (2010) 241 CLR 491, 502, 504, 506.
- **Indirect inconsistency / cover the field**: where the Commonwealth Parliament intended its law to state completely, exhaustively or exclusively what the law is on the relevant subject matter, and the State law enters that same field: *Ex parte McLean* (1930) 43 CLR 472, 483; *Clyde Engineering Co Ltd v Cowburn* (1926) 37 CLR 466, 489.

If direct inconsistency is established, it is unnecessary in principle to rely on indirect inconsistency, although both analyses are often considered: *Dickson v R* (2010) 241 CLR 491, 506–7.

**Application:** A Commonwealth licence does **not automatically** override a Victorian prohibition merely because it is Commonwealth in source. The constitutional question is not resolved by labels such as “licence”, “authorisation” or “approval” alone. The decisive question is the **legal operation** of the Commonwealth law and the Victorian law when read together. The Commonwealth law may simply create a federal permission subject to other valid laws, or it may confer a right, privilege or immunity that a State law cannot significantly cut down. Alternatively, the federal scheme may show an intention to regulate the field exclusively.

So the starting point is to examine the actual statutory text of:
- the Commonwealth Act and any regulations under which the licence was granted;
- the licence conditions themselves; and
- the Victorian Act creating the prohibition.

Without that wording, the answer cannot be categorical.

**Conclusion:** No, the federal licence does **not automatically** override the Victorian prohibition. The result depends on whether the two laws are inconsistent in operation under **s 109**, assessed by direct inconsistency and, if necessary, cover-the-field inconsistency.

### 2. Issue
Whether a **Commonwealth licence or statutory authorisation** amounts to a mere **permission** or instead confers a **right, privilege or immunity** such that a Victorian law prohibiting part of the service would alter, impair or detract from Commonwealth law.

**Rule:** A State law is directly inconsistent with a Commonwealth law if it takes away or interferes with a **right or privilege** conferred by the Commonwealth law: *Clyde Engineering Co Ltd v Cowburn* (1926) 37 CLR 466; *Colvin v Bradley Bros Pty Ltd* (1943) 68 CLR 151; *Blackley v Devondale Cream (Vic) Pty Ltd* (1968) 117 CLR 253. The general test is whether the State law **alters, impairs or detracts** from the operation of Commonwealth law, and that interference must be **significant and not trivial**: *Dickson v R* (2010) 241 CLR 491, 502, 504, 506; *Jemena Asset Management (3) Pty Ltd v Coinvest Ltd* (2011) 244 CLR 508, 525. A State law must not significantly negate the criteria for the existence and adjudication of rights or liabilities under the federal law: *Dickson v R* (2010) 241 CLR 491, 504.

At the same time, not every Commonwealth law that “authorises” conduct necessarily gives the holder a constitutionally protected positive right to act free from State regulation. A State law may still operate if it is merely **complementary or conducive** to the federal law rather than undermining it: *Jemena Asset Management (3) Pty Ltd v Coinvest Ltd* (2011) 244 CLR 508, 521. And a Commonwealth law can expressly indicate that it is **not intended to exclude** concurrent State operation, which may be critical: *Momcilovic v R* (2011) 245 CLR 1, 71, 119–21, 231.

There can also be no objection, in principle, to a Commonwealth law providing that a person is authorised to undertake an activity despite State laws prohibiting, restricting, qualifying or regulating it: *Council of the Municipality of Botany v Federal Airports Corp* (1992) 175 CLR 453, 465. But whether a particular licence has that effect depends on the statute's language and structure.

**Application:** The distinction you raise between a **permission** and a **positive right** is central.

A Commonwealth licence may operate in at least three different ways:

| Commonwealth wording or structure | Likely character | Likely inconsistency consequence |
|---|---|---|
| The law says a person **may** do the activity if licensed, but says nothing about State laws, or indicates State laws continue to apply | Often a **permission within federal law only**, not necessarily a right to act despite State law | Victorian prohibition may still operate unless it significantly impairs the federal scheme |
| The law says a licensee is **authorised notwithstanding any State law** to do the activity | Strong indication of a **right, privilege or immunity** against State restriction | Victorian prohibition is likely inconsistent |
| The law creates a detailed national code for who may do the activity, on what conditions, with what limits and sanctions, suggesting exclusivity | More than a bare permission; may show a **federal right** and/or an intent to **cover the field** | Victorian prohibition is more likely inconsistent |

The practical distinction is this:

- A **mere permission** means the Commonwealth law removes a **federal** prohibition or grants approval for federal purposes, but does not necessarily promise that the activity can be carried on irrespective of State law.
- A **positive right, privilege or immunity** means the Commonwealth law gives the holder legal protection to engage in the conduct, or to be free from inconsistent State restraint, so that a State prohibition would materially cut down what the Commonwealth law grants.

The authorities show that inconsistency is not confined to “must do” versus “must not do”. A State law may be inconsistent even where dual compliance is physically possible, if the State law takes away what the Commonwealth law confers: *Clyde Engineering Co Ltd v Cowburn* (1926) 37 CLR 466; *Blackley v Devondale Cream (Vic) Pty Ltd* (1968) 117 CLR 253; *Dickson v R* (2010) 241 CLR 491.

What would matter most here is whether the federal scheme:
- uses language such as **“authorised”**, **“entitled”**, **“may do so notwithstanding State law”**, or creates an immunity from State liability;
- imposes a comprehensive set of conditions for when the service may be provided;
- contains a clause preserving State laws, or instead suggesting national uniformity; and
- treats the licence as the complete legal warrant for the activity.

**Conclusion:** A Commonwealth licence is **not necessarily** a positive right that displaces State law. But if, on its proper construction, it confers a **right, privilege or immunity**, or is part of an exclusive federal code, a Victorian prohibition on the same activity is more likely to be inconsistent.

### 3. Issue
Whether there is **direct inconsistency** because it is impossible to obey both laws, or because the Victorian law takes away a right, privilege or immunity conferred by Commonwealth law.

**Rule:** Direct inconsistency exists where there is a direct conflict or textual collision between the two laws, including where:
1. **simultaneous obedience is impossible**; or
2. the State law **takes away a right, privilege or immunity** conferred by Commonwealth law; or
3. the State law otherwise **alters, impairs or detracts** from the operation of the federal law in a significant way.

Authorities include *R v Licensing Court of Brisbane; Ex parte Daniell* (1920) 28 CLR 23 for impossibility of simultaneous obedience; *Clyde Engineering Co Ltd v Cowburn* (1926) 37 CLR 466 and *Blackley v Devondale Cream (Vic) Pty Ltd* (1968) 117 CLR 253 for one law taking away a right conferred by another; and *Dickson v R* (2010) 241 CLR 491 and *Jemena Asset Management (3) Pty Ltd v Coinvest Ltd* (2011) 244 CLR 508 for the “alters, impairs or detracts” analysis.

**Application:** On your facts, **impossibility of simultaneous obedience** may or may not exist.

It would exist if the Commonwealth law effectively says you **must** or are legally entitled to perform the relevant part of the service in circumstances where the Victorian law says you **must not**. For example:
- Commonwealth law requires licensees to provide that component to all qualifying customers; and
- Victorian law prohibits provision of that same component in Victoria.

In that scenario, one law can only be obeyed at the cost of disobeying the other.

But often a licence does not oblige conduct; it only permits it. If the Commonwealth law says you **may** provide the service, and the Victorian law says you **must not** provide that part in Victoria, then at a practical level you can obey both by **not providing** that part of the service in Victoria. On that narrower test alone, simultaneous obedience may be possible.

However, that does **not end the inquiry**. Even where you can obey both, there may still be direct inconsistency if the Victorian law takes away a right or privilege conferred by the Commonwealth law. That is the lesson of *Clyde Engineering* and *Blackley*. So the right question is not only “Can I comply with both?” but also “Does the Victorian law materially cut down what the Commonwealth law has granted?”

Relevant factual and textual indicators include:

- Does the Commonwealth law say the licence holder is **authorised to carry on the service throughout Australia**?
- Does it define the service, and does the prohibited Victorian component fall squarely within that federally authorised service?
- Does the Commonwealth law impose national conditions and national safety or consumer standards for that component?
- Does the Victorian law prohibit the same conduct that the federal licence contemplates as lawful?
- Is the Victorian law aimed at the same legal subject matter, or only at some collateral matter?

If the Victorian law merely regulates a different aspect in a way that is **complementary** to the federal scheme, inconsistency is less likely: *Jemena Asset Management (3) Pty Ltd v Coinvest Ltd* (2011) 244 CLR 508, 521. If instead it substantially narrows the federally authorised activity, inconsistency is more likely.

**Conclusion:** **Possible compliance with both laws does not by itself defeat inconsistency.** If the Victorian law significantly curtails a right, privilege or immunity conferred by the Commonwealth law, there may still be direct inconsistency even though you could avoid breach by not acting.

### 4. Issue
Whether there is **indirect inconsistency** because the Commonwealth law was intended to regulate the relevant subject matter exclusively, completely or exhaustively, leaving no room for Victorian regulation of that part of the service.

**Rule:** Indirect inconsistency arises where the Commonwealth Parliament intends its law to be the complete, exhaustive or exclusive statement of the law on the relevant subject matter, and a State law seeks to regulate that same subject matter: *Ex parte McLean* (1930) 43 CLR 472, 483; *Clyde Engineering Co Ltd v Cowburn* (1926) 37 CLR 466, 489. The issue is one of statutory construction: has the Commonwealth **covered the field**?

Examples in the authorities include:
- exclusive federal regulation of meat preparation for export displacing State regulation: *O’Sullivan v Noarlunga Meat Ltd* (1954) 92 CLR 565; *O’Sullivan v Noarlunga Meat Ltd* (1956) 95 CLR 177;
- exhaustive federal specification of dismissed employees’ rights displacing State law: *Metal Trades Industry Assn of Australia v Amalgamated Metal Workers’ and Shipwrights’ Union* (1983) 152 CLR 632;
- exclusive federal regulation of aspects of air transport safety displacing overlapping State regulation while leaving non-overlapping parts operative: *Airlines of New South Wales Pty Ltd v New South Wales (Airlines Case) (No 2)* (1965) 113 CLR 54;
- comprehensive federal regulation of civil aviation safety leading to inconsistency with State prosecutions: *Heli-Aust Pty Ltd v Cahill* (2011) 194 FCR 502.

**Application:** Even if the licence is not itself a positive right overriding State law, the Commonwealth statutory scheme may still imply exclusivity.

Facts and wording suggesting the Commonwealth intended to **cover the field** would include:
- a **detailed national licensing regime** for this exact service;
- exhaustive criteria for grant, suspension, cancellation, conditions, and enforcement;
- detailed federal standards governing how the prohibited component is to be provided;
- a purpose of ensuring **national uniformity**;
- provisions showing Commonwealth regulators decide when and on what terms the service may be offered;
- express language such as “exclusive”, “complete code”, or “despite any State law”;
- a licensing structure where State variation would undermine the national scheme.

Facts suggesting the Commonwealth **did not** cover the field would include:
- express preservation of State laws;
- Commonwealth conditions framed as minimum standards only;
- clear allowance for concurrent State regulation;
- a Victorian law aimed at a genuinely distinct subject matter;
- a scheme whose structure assumes parallel State controls.

Your phrase that the Commonwealth licence authorises the service “throughout Australia” is relevant but **not conclusive**. The State law otherwise continues to exist, though its operative force is suspended while the Commonwealth law remains in force: *Cockatoo Docks & Engineering Co Pty Ltd v Lamb* [1961] NSWR 197, 204. “Invalid” here means **inoperative**, and the State law may revive if the Commonwealth law later disappears: *Butler v A-G (Vic)* (1961) 106 CLR 268, 283.

**Application:** If only one component of your service is prohibited by Victoria, and only that component conflicts with federal law, then ordinarily only that part of the Victorian law would be inoperative as applied to that component. The rest of the Victorian statute may remain valid and operative.

If, however, the State provisions are so interdependent that severance would distort the statute beyond what Parliament intended, the inoperative effect may extend further. That is usually a harder argument, and the burden on the party asserting that separation is impossible is not light: *Wenn v A-G (Vic)* (1948) 77 CLR 84.

**Conclusion:** If inconsistency is shown, the Victorian law is displaced **only so far as it conflicts** with the Commonwealth law. It is not automatically struck down in its entirety.
