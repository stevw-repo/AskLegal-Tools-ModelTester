**Response**

## Legal Issues Identified

1. **Whether**, under **Commonwealth Constitution s 109**, the Victorian prohibition is inconsistent with the Commonwealth licensing regime so that the Victorian law is inoperative to the extent of the inconsistency.

2. **Whether** the Commonwealth licence is merely a **permission** to engage in the service, or instead confers a **right, privilege or immunity** that a Victorian law cannot significantly impair, detract from or take away.

3. **Whether** compliance with both laws is legally possible, and if so, whether inconsistency may still arise because the Commonwealth law was intended to regulate the relevant subject matter **exhaustively or exclusively**.

4. **To what extent** any inconsistency would affect the Victorian law.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Whether, under Commonwealth Constitution s 109, the Victorian prohibition is inconsistent with the Commonwealth licensing regime so that the Victorian law is inoperative to the extent of the inconsistency

**Issue:**  
Whether a Victorian law prohibiting one part of a service authorised by a Commonwealth licence is inconsistent with Commonwealth law for the purposes of **Commonwealth Constitution s 109**.

**Rule:**  
State law must give way where it is inconsistent with Commonwealth law: **Commonwealth Constitution s 109**. Inconsistency may arise in two main ways.

First, there may be **direct inconsistency**. That occurs where there is a direct conflict or textual collision between the laws, where they make contradictory provision on the same topic, where simultaneous obedience is impossible, or where the State law takes away a right, privilege or immunity conferred by Commonwealth law: *University of Wollongong v Metwally* (1984) 158 CLR 447, 455–6; *Miller v Miller* (1978) 141 CLR 269, 275; *R v Credit Tribunal; Ex parte General Motors Acceptance Corp, Australia* (1977) 137 CLR 545, 563; *Clyde Engineering Co Ltd v Cowburn* (1926) 37 CLR 466, 490; *R v Licensing Court of Brisbane; Ex parte Daniell* (1920) 28 CLR 23; *Blackley v Devondale Cream (Vic) Pty Ltd* (1968) 117 CLR 253. A State law will also be directly inconsistent if it **alters, impairs or detracts** from the operation of Commonwealth law in a way that is **significant and not trivial**: *Dickson v R* (2010) 241 CLR 491, 502, 504, 506; *Jemena Asset Management (3) Pty Ltd v Coinvest Ltd* (2011) 244 CLR 508, 525.

Secondly, there may be **indirect inconsistency** or **covering the field**. That occurs where the Commonwealth Parliament intends its law to state completely, exhaustively or exclusively what the law is on the relevant subject, and the State law attempts to regulate the same conduct or matter: *Ex parte McLean* (1930) 43 CLR 472, 483; *Clyde Engineering Co Ltd v Cowburn* (1926) 37 CLR 466, 489. In identifying that field, the starting point is the intended scope and operation of the Commonwealth law; if the two laws are directed to different subject matters, there is no indirect inconsistency: *P v P* (1994) 181 CLR 583, 601; *R v Winneke; Ex parte Gallagher* (1982) 152 CLR 211, 218.

If direct inconsistency is established, it is unnecessary in principle to rely on indirect inconsistency, although both analyses are often considered because they overlap: *Dickson v R* (2010) 241 CLR 491, 506–7.

**Application:**  
A Commonwealth licence does **not automatically** override a Victorian prohibition simply because it exists. The constitutional question is not whether the Commonwealth has regulated the area in a general sense, but whether the legal operation of the Commonwealth law and the Victorian law is inconsistent.

If the Commonwealth law simply establishes a national licensing scheme and says, in substance, that a person may be licensed if certain criteria are met, that alone may not answer whether Victoria can still prohibit a sub-activity. The court would compare:

- the **rights, immunities and obligations** created by the Commonwealth law and licence; and
- the **effect** of the Victorian prohibition on those rights, immunities and obligations.

If the Commonwealth law is framed so that the licence holder is **authorised to provide the service notwithstanding State law**, or the Commonwealth regime appears intended to be the **exclusive legal code** governing whether that service may be provided, inconsistency is much more likely. On the other hand, if the Commonwealth law is concerned with one subject only — for example, technical qualifications, safety, or market entry — and the Victorian law addresses a different subject — for example, land use, local amenity, or some separate aspect of consumer protection — both may operate together.

The user’s facts suggest a **partial overlap**: the Victorian law prohibits only **one part** of the service. That does not prevent inconsistency. The High Court has recognised that inconsistency may exist only in relation to some aspects of a field, leaving the rest of the State law operative: *Airlines of New South Wales Pty Ltd v New South Wales (Airlines Case) (No 2)* (1965) 113 CLR 54. Equally, if the Commonwealth law regulates only some aspects of the service and Victoria regulates other aspects, there may be no inconsistency at all: *Commercial Radio Coffs Harbour Ltd v Fuller* (1986) 161 CLR 47, 56–7.

So the federal licence does **not** automatically prevail. The result depends on the **text, structure and purpose** of the Commonwealth statute and licence, and on the **precise subject matter** and legal effect of the Victorian prohibition.

**Conclusion:**  
The federal licence will override the Victorian prohibition **only if** the Victorian law is inconsistent with Commonwealth law under **Commonwealth Constitution s 109**. That requires close analysis of the operative statutory language and the field each law regulates. There is no automatic rule that every Commonwealth licence displaces every inconsistent-looking State restriction.

### 2. Whether the Commonwealth licence is merely a permission to engage in the service, or instead confers a right, privilege or immunity that a Victorian law cannot significantly impair, detract from or take away

**Issue:**  
Whether the Commonwealth licence merely permits conduct, or whether it confers a **right, privilege or immunity** such that a Victorian prohibition would directly conflict by taking that benefit away.

**Rule:**  
A State law is directly inconsistent where it takes away or interferes with a **right or privilege** conferred by Commonwealth law: *Clyde Engineering Co Ltd v Cowburn* (1926) 37 CLR 466; *Blackley v Devondale Cream (Vic) Pty Ltd* (1968) 117 CLR 253. The broader test is whether the State law **alters, impairs or detracts** from the Commonwealth law’s operation, and that impairment must be **significant and not trivial**: *Dickson v R* (2010) 241 CLR 491, 502, 504, 506; *Jemena Asset Management (3) Pty Ltd v Coinvest Ltd* (2011) 244 CLR 508, 525.

The authorities also recognise that a Commonwealth law may validly provide that a person is **authorised** to undertake an activity despite a State law prohibiting, restricting, qualifying or regulating that activity: *Council of the Municipality of Botany v Federal Airports Corp* (1992) 175 CLR 453, 465. Conversely, a State law may survive if it is merely **complementary or conducive** to the Commonwealth scheme rather than undermining it: *Jemena Asset Management (3) Pty Ltd v Coinvest Ltd* (2011) 244 CLR 508, 521.

**Application:**  
The distinction you raise between a **permission** and a **positive right** is central.

A Commonwealth licence can operate at different levels:

| Possible Commonwealth effect | Likely constitutional consequence |
|---|---|
| **Mere permission**: the licence means the holder is not in breach of Commonwealth law if they provide the service | A State prohibition may still operate, unless the Commonwealth law shows an intention to exclude State control or the State law significantly impairs a federal privilege |
| **Right/privilege/immunity**: the licence entitles the holder to provide the service, or protects them from State interference | A Victorian prohibition is more likely to be directly inconsistent because it removes or significantly detracts from that federally conferred position |
| **Exclusive federal code**: the Commonwealth scheme appears exhaustive as to when and how the service may be provided | A Victorian law regulating the same subject is more likely to be invalid under cover-the-field inconsistency |

In practical terms, a court would look for statutory wording of these kinds:

- language that the licence holder **“may”** provide the service, without more;
- stronger language that the holder is **“authorised”**, **“entitled”**, or **“may do so despite any law of a State”**;
- provisions showing whether State laws are **preserved**, **excluded**, or expected to operate concurrently;
- the breadth of the Commonwealth scheme: whether it deals comprehensively with eligibility, conditions, supervision, enforcement, suspension, exemptions and sanctions.

A Commonwealth **permission** is not necessarily weak. The authorities show that even where simultaneous obedience is possible, inconsistency can still arise if the State law takes away a federal privilege. But the stronger and more explicit the Commonwealth conferral, the easier it is to show that a State prohibition **alters, impairs or detracts** from it.

For example, if the licence says the holder is authorised to provide the entire regulated service throughout Australia, subject only to Commonwealth conditions, that points toward a federally conferred privilege. If the Victorian law then bans one component that the Commonwealth scheme specifically contemplates and regulates, Victoria may be taking away part of that federal privilege.

By contrast, if the licence simply means the holder has satisfied federal minimum requirements, and the Commonwealth statute is silent about State laws or concurrent regulation, the licence may be characterised as a **necessary federal approval** but not a positive right to operate free from State restrictions.

**Conclusion:**  
A Commonwealth licence does **not necessarily** create a freestanding positive right against State law. The key question is whether the Commonwealth law and licence, properly construed, confer a **right, privilege or immunity** that the Victorian prohibition significantly removes or undermines. The statutory language and overall scheme are decisive.

### 3. Whether compliance with both laws is legally possible, and if so, whether inconsistency may still arise because the Commonwealth law was intended to regulate the relevant subject matter exhaustively or exclusively

**Issue:**  
Whether the fact that you could potentially comply with both laws by not performing the prohibited part of the service prevents inconsistency.

**Rule:**  
One recognised form of direct inconsistency is where simultaneous obedience is impossible: *R v Licensing Court of Brisbane; Ex parte Daniell* (1920) 28 CLR 23. Two laws are inconsistent where each imposes a duty and one can only be obeyed by disobeying the other: *R v Licensing Court of Brisbane; Ex parte Daniell* (1920) 28 CLR 23, 29.

However, impossibility of simultaneous obedience is **not the only test**. A State law may still be inconsistent even where both laws can literally be obeyed, if the State law takes away a right or privilege conferred by Commonwealth law: *Clyde Engineering Co Ltd v Cowburn* (1926) 37 CLR 466; *Blackley v Devondale Cream (Vic) Pty Ltd* (1968) 117 CLR 253. Likewise, inconsistency may arise where the Commonwealth intended to **cover the field** completely, exhaustively or exclusively: *Ex parte McLean* (1930) 43 CLR 472, 483; *Clyde Engineering Co Ltd v Cowburn* (1926) 37 CLR 466, 489.

The field inquiry begins with the intended scope and operation of the Commonwealth law. If the State law addresses a **different subject matter**, there is no indirect inconsistency: *P v P* (1994) 181 CLR 583, 601; *Commercial Radio Coffs Harbour Ltd v Fuller* (1986) 161 CLR 47, 56–7; *New South Wales v Commonwealth (Health Fund Levy Case)* (1983) 151 CLR 302, 316, 319, 334.

**Application:**  
On your facts, it may well be **possible** to comply with both laws in a practical sense: you could hold the federal licence but refrain from the part of the service Victorian law prohibits. If that is all that is meant, then the strict **Daniell** impossibility test may not be satisfied.

But that does **not end the matter**.

The more difficult question is whether the Victorian prohibition would:

1. **take away a federal privilege** to conduct that part of the service; or
2. intrude into a field the Commonwealth intended to regulate **exhaustively**.

That is why a Commonwealth permission can sometimes prevail even though literal dual compliance is possible. *Clyde Engineering* is the classic example: a State law may impose a higher or different standard that a person can obey, yet still be inconsistent because it strips away the federal liberty or privilege that Commonwealth law created.

So, in your scenario:

- If the Commonwealth law says, in effect, “a licensed provider may provide service X, including component Y”, and Victoria says “component Y must not be provided in Victoria”, both laws may be literally obeyed by omitting Y — but Victoria may still be taking away the federal privilege to provide Y.
- If the Commonwealth law instead says only that a person may be licensed subject to federal standards, and says nothing suggesting State restrictions are excluded, then the fact that both laws can be obeyed becomes more important, because it points away from direct contradiction.

The **cover-the-field** analysis is often decisive in national licensing schemes. Factors suggesting the Commonwealth meant to regulate the field exclusively include:

- a detailed and comprehensive code;
- carefully specified licence conditions and exceptions;
- Commonwealth enforcement mechanisms;
- a national uniformity objective;
- provisions indicating that Commonwealth approval is sufficient authority to undertake the activity;
- wording inconsistent with additional State prohibitions.

Factors suggesting no exclusive field include:

- express preservation of State laws;
- a scheme directed only to one aspect of the activity, such as safety, technical competence, or minimum standards;
- room left for State regulation of different aspects;
- a subject matter which, on proper characterisation, differs from that of the Victorian law.

**Conclusion:**  
The fact that you can probably comply with both laws by not performing the prohibited part does **not** by itself defeat a s 109 argument. Simultaneous obedience is only one pathway. A Victorian prohibition may still be invalid if it significantly impairs a federal privilege or intrudes into a field the Commonwealth intended to occupy exclusively.

### 4. To what extent any inconsistency would affect the Victorian law

**Issue:**  
If inconsistency is established, whether the whole Victorian law fails or only the inconsistent part.

**Rule:**  
Where inconsistency is found, the State law is invalid only **to the extent of the inconsistency**, and is otherwise valid: **Commonwealth Constitution s 109**; *Butler v A-G (Vic)* (1961) 106 CLR 268, 283. In this context, “invalid” means **inoperative**, so the State law may revive if the inconsistency later disappears: *Butler v A-G (Vic)* (1961) 106 CLR 268, 283. The authorities also show that where only some parts of a State law regulate the same subject matter as an exclusive Commonwealth regime, only those parts are displaced, while other parts may remain operative: *Airlines of New South Wales Pty Ltd v New South Wales (Airlines Case) (No 2)* (1965) 113 CLR 54.

**Application:**  
Because your concern is about only **one part** of the regulated service, the most likely constitutional outcome — if inconsistency is established — is **partial inoperability** only. That is, the Victorian law would remain effective generally, but could not validly prohibit that part of the service to the extent the prohibition conflicts with Commonwealth law.

This also means that a court would need to define the overlap precisely:

- What exact conduct does the Commonwealth licence authorise?
- What exact conduct does the Victorian law prohibit?
- Are they directed to the same legal subject, or to different subjects?
- Is the Commonwealth law exclusive as to that precise aspect only, or broader?

The narrower and more specific the overlap, the more likely the result is a narrow declaration that the Victorian prohibition is inoperative only as applied to licensed conduct of a particular kind.

**Conclusion:**  
Even if your s 109 argument succeeds, the Victorian law would usually be displaced **only to the extent of the inconsistency**, not struck down wholesale.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Ownership of copyright in the app source code and graphics where the agreement is silent

**Issue:** Under Australian law, who is likely to own copyright in source code and graphics created by a **contractor** for your company where the contract describes the work and fees but does not expressly assign copyright?

**Rule:** As a general principle of Australian copyright law, **copyright initially belongs to the author/creator**, subject to specific statutory exceptions and any effective contractual assignment. For **independent contractors**, the default position is generally different from employees: absent an express assignment or a statutory exception, the contractor commonly remains the copyright owner. A contract may, however, give rise to an **express or implied licence** permitting the client to use the work. The existence, content and meaning of any licence are determined by **general contract principles**. A **licence** may be express or implied from conduct, and **non-exclusive licences** may be written, oral or implied; **exclusive licences** must be in writing. A licence allows a person to do an act that would otherwise infringe copyright: *Concrete Pty Ltd v Parramatta Design & Developments Pty Ltd* (2006) 229 CLR 577; *Copyright Agency Ltd v New South Wales* (2008) 233 CLR 279; *Acohs Pty Ltd v Ucorp Pty Ltd* (2012) 201 FCR 173; *Elecon Australia Pty Ltd v PIV Drives GmBH* [2010] FCAFC 56; *Kovan Engineering (Aust) Pty Ltd v Gold Peg International Pty Ltd* (2006) 234 ALR 241. The *Copyright Act 1968* (Cth) also recognises licences granted by owners and prospective owners: *Copyright Act 1968* (Cth) ss 196(4), 197(3).

**Application:** On your facts, the contractor appears to have **created** the source code and graphics. Because the agreement says nothing about **copyright ownership**, the first risk for your company is that the contractor may still own some or all of the copyright unless there are other documents, onboarding terms, platform terms, emails, statements of work, or later conduct showing an assignment.  

That said, the written agreement describing the work and fees may still support at least an **implied non-exclusive licence** allowing your company to use the app for the purpose for which it was commissioned. Whether that implied licence is narrow or broad depends on the contract’s construction: for example, whether the app was commissioned for your company’s sole commercial use, whether the contractor was paid to build a bespoke product for your business, whether you received source files and deployment access, and whether both sides acted on the basis that your company could exploit the app commercially.

A critical distinction is this:

| Scenario | Likely position |
|---|---|
| Contractor retained copyright, but your company received only an implied licence to use the app | Your company may use within the licence scope, but may have **limited standing** to sue for primary infringement unless it is an exclusive licensee or owner |

### 2. Whether the copying and sale of the competing app is likely to amount to copyright infringement in Australia

**Issue:** Under **Commonwealth** law, does a former contractor who copied source code and graphics from your company’s app and sells a competing version online likely infringe copyright?

**Rule:** In general, a person infringes copyright by doing, in Australia and without licence, an act comprised in the copyright: *Copyright Act 1968* (Cth) ss 13(1), 31, 36(1), 101(1). Dealing with a **substantial part** may also infringe: *Copyright Act 1968* (Cth) s 14. Copyright in an artistic work is infringed by a person who exercises, or authorises the exercise of, the copyright owner’s rights in Australia without authority: *Copyright Act 1968* (Cth) s 36.  

For **reproduction**, there must be some **causal connection** between the copyright work and the alleged infringing work; independent creation does not infringe: *SW Hart & Co Pty Ltd v Edwards Hot Water Systems* (1985) 159 CLR 466; *Tamawood Ltd v Habitare Developments Pty Ltd (admin apptd) (recs and mgrs apptd)* (2015) 112 IPR 439; *Vawdrey Australia Pty Ltd v Krueger Transport Equipment Pty Ltd* (2009) 261 ALR 269.  

In relation to **artistic works**, the test for reproduction involves a **visual comparison** between the original and alleged copy. Mere use of a similar style, colours, subject matter or technique does not infringe unless there is actual reproduction of the work: *LB (Plastics) Ltd v Swish Products Ltd* [1979] FSR 145; *SW Hart & Co Pty Ltd v Edwards Hot Water Systems* (1985) 159 CLR 466; *Cummins v Vella* [2002] FCAFC 218. More broadly, copyright protects the **expression** of an idea, not merely the idea, scheme, system or method itself: *Autodesk Inc v Dyason* (1992) 173 CLR 330; *Autodesk Inc v Dyason (No 2)* (1993) 176 CLR 300; *Blackie & Sons Ltd v Lothian Book Publishing Co Pty Ltd* (1921) 29 CLR 396.

The main qualification is **ownership/standing**. ### 3. Whether any licence may have arisen from the agreement or conduct, and how far it extends

**Issue:** Under Australian law, did the agreement or the parties’ conduct create a **licence**, and could that licence affect whether the contractor’s conduct is wrongful?

A licence may be an **express or implied** term of a contract. **Non-exclusive licences** may be written, oral, or implied from conduct, while **exclusive licences** must be in writing. The existence, content and meaning of the licence are determined by **general contract principles**: *Concrete Pty Ltd v Parramatta Design & Developments Pty Ltd* (2006) 229 CLR 577; *Copyright Agency Ltd v New South Wales* (2008) 233 CLR 279; *JR Consulting & Drafting Pty Ltd v Cummings* [2014] NSWSC 1252; *Elecon Australia Pty Ltd v PIV Drives GmBH* [2010] FCAFC 56; *Software AG (Aust) Pty Ltd v Racing and Wagering Western Australia* (2009) 175 FCR 121; *Kovan Engineering (Aust) Pty Ltd v Gold Peg International Pty Ltd* (2006) 234 ALR 241.

**Application:** Even if your company does not own copyright, the written agreement almost certainly supports at least some **licence** for your company to use the app it paid to have built. **Conclusion:** A **licence** almost certainly exists in some form, but its **scope** is likely to be contested. ## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Whether a Queensland court is bound by the broader High Court authority on a common-law duty question, even though the High Court did not decide the precise factual scenario now in issue

**Issue:** In Queensland, on a **common-law** negligence duty issue, is the court bound by the **High Court of Australia’s** reasoning and controlling principle even if the High Court has not decided the exact factual permutation now before the court?

**Rule:** The **High Court** is the final appellate court for Australia, and its authoritative statements of common-law principle bind all Australian courts. That includes Queensland courts. A lower court is not free to adopt an intermediate appellate approach that is inconsistent with the legal principle established by the High Court merely because the High Court case arose on different facts. The real question is whether the High Court decision establishes a governing principle broad enough to cover the present case by legal analogy. If it does, that principle must be applied. If the High Court authority is genuinely distinguishable and leaves the precise point open, lower courts may reason by analogy from it, but cannot depart from it.

**Application:** On your facts, the High Court decision appears to be **broader** but not directly on the exact duty situation. That means the Queensland court will first ask whether the High Court has already supplied the controlling framework for recognising or denying the alleged duty. If the NSW and Victorian appellate decisions represent competing applications of that broader framework, the Queensland court cannot simply choose whichever intermediate court it prefers in the abstract; it must choose the approach that best accords with the High Court’s reasoning, method, and limits on incremental development of the law.

**Conclusion:** The **High Court decision is binding to the extent of the principle it establishes**. The absence of an exact factual match does not prevent it from governing. The Queensland court should treat the High Court authority as the primary source of law and use the interstate appellate decisions only to help work out how that principle should be applied to the facts now before it.

### 2. What persuasive weight a Queensland court should give to conflicting interstate intermediate appellate decisions on the common law of Australia, namely a recent unanimous New South Wales Court of Appeal decision and an older Victorian Court of Appeal decision

**Issue:** In Queensland, what weight should be given to conflicting **interstate Courts of Appeal** on a common-law question?

**Rule:** The **common law of Australia is a single body of law**, not a separate common law for each State. For that reason, an intermediate appellate decision from another State on a pure common-law question is not binding in Queensland, but it carries substantial **persuasive** weight, especially where it is directly on point. As a matter of judicial comity and national coherence, a court will usually give serious respect to such a decision unless convinced it is wrong, inconsistent with High Court authority, or outweighed by stronger reasoning in another appellate decision. Relevant considerations include:
- whether the issue is one of **general common law** rather than local statute;
- the **court level** of the competing decisions;
- whether the decision is **unanimous**;
- the degree of **factual similarity**;
- the quality of the **reasoning**;
- its **consistency with High Court doctrine**; and
- where otherwise comparable, **recency**.

A recent unanimous interstate appellate decision on nearly identical facts will ordinarily be influential. An older interstate appellate decision pointing the other way remains important, but may carry less persuasive force if its reasoning sits less comfortably with later High Court developments or with modern doctrine.

**Application:** Here, both the NSW and Victorian decisions are from intermediate appellate courts and so neither is binding on a Queensland court. However, the **recent unanimous NSW Court of Appeal decision on almost identical facts** is likely to attract stronger persuasive weight than the **older Victorian Court of Appeal decision**, at least initially, because it is more recent, factually closer, and apparently represents a clear collegiate view. That said, its persuasive force weakens materially if:
- it is inconsistent with the broader High Court decision;
- it applies duty principles too expansively or narrowly compared with the High Court’s method; or
- the Victorian decision, though older, better reflects the High Court’s controlling approach.

So the contest is not resolved by counting courts or by recency alone. The decisive question is which interstate decision is **more faithful to the High Court and to orthodox common-law reasoning**.

**Conclusion:** Neither interstate appellate decision binds the Queensland court. Both are **persuasive**, but the **recent unanimous NSW decision on nearly identical facts would usually start with greater persuasive force** than the older Victorian decision. Even so, the Queensland court should prefer the Victorian approach if it better accords with binding High Court principle.

### 3. How a Queensland court should resolve that conflict consistently with the single common law of Australia

**Issue:** How should a Queensland court resolve conflicting interstate appellate authority on a novel or unsettled common-law duty issue?

**Rule:** Because the common law is national, a Queensland court should aim for **coherence and uniformity** across Australia. The proper approach is:
1. identify the **binding High Court principles** governing duty of care;
2. determine whether one interstate appellate decision is **more consistent** with those principles;
3. if the High Court has not squarely resolved the point, prefer the approach that develops the law **incrementally and by analogy**, rather than by broad policy assertion;
4. give substantial respect to a closely analogous interstate appellate decision, particularly from an intermediate appellate court, unless persuaded it is plainly wrong; and
5. avoid creating unnecessary divergence in Australian common law unless compelled by stronger authority or reasoning.

If the competing interstate decisions remain evenly balanced, a Queensland court may adopt the view it considers legally preferable, but should explain why that approach best promotes doctrinal consistency with the High Court and the national common law.

**Application:** In your scenario, the likely sequence is:
- The Queensland court will begin with the **High Court framework** on duty.
- It will then test both the NSW and Victorian decisions against that framework.
- The **NSW decision** has practical advantages: it is recent, unanimous, and factually almost identical. That makes it a strong candidate for adoption in the interests of national consistency.
- But if the **Victorian decision** better reflects the logic, limits, and caution in the High Court’s broader duty analysis, the Queensland court should follow the Victorian reasoning instead, despite its age.
- If the High Court authority materially points in one direction, that will usually settle the matter.
- If the High Court leaves genuine room for choice, the court will often be reluctant to create fresh divergence from a recent, well-reasoned interstate appellate decision on the same facts without a clear reason.

In practical terms, the stronger argument is usually framed this way:
- **For the NSW side:** national coherence, factual closeness, unanimity, and modern articulation of the law.
- **For your side:** the NSW decision should not be followed if it overreads or departs from the governing High Court principle, and the Victorian decision is preferable because it is doctrinally truer to that principle.

**Conclusion:** The conflict should be resolved by asking **which interstate appellate approach best implements the binding High Court principles within the single common law of Australia**. If both are genuinely open on the High Court authorities, the Queensland court is likely to give considerable weight to the **recent unanimous NSW decision on identical facts**, but it is not obliged to follow it if persuaded that the **Victorian decision is the sounder application of High Court doctrine**.
