Yes — **a binding contract may already have been formed**, even though the agreement was made over **WhatsApp**, if the essential terms were agreed: the **item** (the camera), the **price** (**S$700**), and the seller **accepted** your offer.

If so, the seller’s decision to sell the camera to someone else is **likely a breach of contract**.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

**Issue:** Whether a WhatsApp agreement to buy a second-hand camera is legally enforceable.

**Rule:** Under Singapore law, a contract can be formed through messages if there is a clear **offer**, **acceptance**, and an intention to be bound. A sale of goods contract does not generally need to be in a special form just because it was concluded informally.

**Application:** On your facts, you offered **S$700**, the seller accepted, and the subject matter appears sufficiently identified as a **second-hand camera**. That points toward a concluded contract. The seller then selling to another buyer would generally amount to a failure to perform that contract.

**Conclusion:** You likely have a **claim for breach of contract**.

- the difference between the **contract price** and the **market price** of a comparable camera, if replacement now costs more; and

If you had already paid money, you would also usually be entitled to a **refund**.

If the camera is genuinely rare or unique, a stronger argument may exist for an order requiring delivery, but for most second-hand consumer goods, the court would usually treat **damages as adequate**.

I paid a Singapore caterer a S$1,200 deposit for my wedding, but I cancelled and they refuse to return any of it. What should I do?

## Legal Issues Identified

1. Whether the **S$1,200** was a **true deposit** that the caterer can keep.
2. Whether it was instead a **part payment / advance payment** that may have to be refunded, wholly or partly.
3. Whether the caterer has a contractual right to **forfeit** the sum after your cancellation.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### Issue 1: Does the caterer automatically get to keep the deposit because you cancelled?

**Rule:** Under Singapore law, whether a sum is forfeitable depends first on the **contract** and the **nature of the payment**. A true deposit is generally treated as **earnest money** to secure performance and may be forfeited if the customer backs out. But not every payment labelled a “deposit” is automatically forfeitable. The court looks at whether the sum is **reasonable as an earnest** and what the parties objectively intended.

The general approach from the cases is:

1. Is there a **contractual right to forfeit** the sum?
2. If yes, is the sum a **true deposit**?
3. If it is **not** a true deposit, recovery is considered under general law, including whether the basis for payment failed.

This approach is reflected in **Hon Chin Kong v Yip Fook Mun & Anor [2017] SGHC 286** and clarified by the Court of Appeal in **Li Jialin & Anor v Wingcrown Investment Pte Ltd [2024] SGCA 48**.

**Application:** On your facts alone, the caterer does **not** automatically have the right to keep the S$1,200 just because you cancelled. The key questions are:

- What do the booking terms say about **cancellation** and **refunds**?
- Was the S$1,200 expressed to be **non-refundable**?
- What was the **total contract price**?  
  This matters because whether a sum is a true deposit often depends in part on whether it is a **moderate and reasonable proportion** of the overall price.
- Had the caterer already incurred costs, reserved the date, rejected other bookings, or begun preparation?

Without those facts, it cannot be said conclusively that the whole sum is forfeitable.

**Conclusion:** The caterer can keep the money only if the contract and the surrounding facts support the view that it was a **valid forfeitable deposit** or that they are otherwise entitled to retain some or all of it.

---

### Issue 2: If the contract calls it a “deposit”, does that settle the matter?

**Rule:** No. The court looks at **substance over label**. A payment described as a deposit may still be treated differently if, in reality, it is an advance payment or is unreasonably large. In **Citiraya Technology Sdn Bhd (in liquidation) v Centillion Environment & Recycling Ltd [2008] SGHC 183**, the court distinguished between a **deposit** and a **staged payment**, and stressed that the contract wording and commercial context matter.

**Application:** If your S$1,200 was simply the first instalment of the catering price, rather than earnest money intended to secure the booking, that may support an argument that it was a **part payment** rather than a true deposit. If so, the caterer may not be entitled to keep all of it automatically.

**Conclusion:** Calling it a “deposit” helps the caterer, but it is **not conclusive**.

---

### Issue 3: Can the caterer keep the whole sum even if their actual loss is lower?

**Rule:** A true deposit may be forfeited even if actual loss is lower, but only if it is truly a **reasonable earnest**. If the sum is not a true deposit, the caterer usually cannot just retain it without justification. The Court of Appeal in **Li Jialin & Anor v Wingcrown Investment Pte Ltd [2024] SGCA 48** emphasised that the question is whether the amount is **objectively justifiable by reference to the proper function of a deposit**.

**Application:** In a wedding catering context, relevant facts would include:

- how far in advance you cancelled;
- whether the caterer had committed ingredients, labour, venue coordination, or subcontractors;
- whether the date was a peak date and the caterer lost the chance to take another booking;
- whether S$1,200 is a small or large percentage of the total package price.

If you cancelled **very early** and the caterer suffered little or no real booking-related prejudice, you may have a stronger argument that retaining the entire sum is excessive unless the contract clearly permits it and the amount is a genuine deposit.

**Conclusion:** The caterer may be entitled to keep **some**, **all**, or **none** of the S$1,200 depending on the contract terms and the commercial justification for the sum.

If they still refuse, the practical next step is usually to consider a **Small Claims Tribunals** claim, if the matter falls within its jurisdiction and monetary limits. For a S$1,200 dispute, that is often the most proportionate route.

Your argument would usually be along these lines:

| Your possible argument | Caterer’s possible response |

| The S$1,200 was only an advance payment | It was a true non-refundable booking deposit |
| The contract does not clearly allow forfeiture | The terms expressly say cancellation causes forfeiture |

| The caterer suffered little or no loss | They turned away other customers / incurred preparation costs |

### Risk Assessment
Your prospects depend heavily on:

- **when you cancelled**.

Broadly:
- If the contract clearly says the sum is a **non-refundable deposit**, and S$1,200 is a **modest percentage** of the total wedding catering bill, the caterer may have a stronger position.
- If there is **no clear forfeiture clause**, or S$1,200 looks more like an **advance instalment**, or you cancelled **well before** any real preparation, your refund position improves.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Was this a loan or a gift?

**Issue:**  
Can you prove that the S$25,000 was a loan even though there was no signed loan agreement?

**Rule:**  
Under Singapore law, a loan does not need to be in writing if the evidence shows that money was advanced with an **obligation to repay**. The lender bears the burden of proving the existence of the loan and its terms. Courts look closely at **contemporaneous evidence**, including bank records, messages, and conduct after payment. Where a defendant says the money was a **gift**, the court will assess which version is more credible in light of the documents and surrounding circumstances.

The cases show that:
- an **oral loan** can be proved by documentary and surrounding evidence: **Sinniah Karupaiah v Kumanaruban Rasiah [2021] SGHC 85**;
- the lender must prove the alleged loan with **clear evidence**, and vague or incomplete evidence can be fatal: **Ding Lik Sing v Chow Wai Shuen Mark Francis [2022] SGHC 282**;
- the essence of a loan is the **obligation to repay**, whether on demand or at a future time: **Civil Law Act - Civil Law Act - s 6, s 7**.

**Application:**  
On your facts, the evidence appears **favourable** to you:

- the **bank transfer was labelled as a loan**;
- the messages say he would **repay after selling his flat**;
- the flat was sold **three months ago**, so the agreed trigger for repayment appears to have happened;
- he has already made a **partial repayment of S$2,000**, which is usually more consistent with a loan than a gift.

His current assertion through a mutual friend that it was a gift is likely to be treated as a **bare denial**, unless he has evidence supporting that version. In practice, a court will likely compare:
- your transfer record,
- your messages,
- any acknowledgments by him,
- the fact of the flat sale,
- and the **S$2,000 repayment**.

That partial repayment is especially important because it tends to support the existence of an outstanding debt.

**Conclusion:**  
Based on the facts you gave, you appear to have a **reasonably arguable claim** that this was a loan, not a gift.

A loan may be repayable on a specified event or at a future time. If the agreed repayment trigger occurs, the borrower is expected to repay according to that arrangement.

**Application:**  
Your messages reportedly say repayment would be made **after receiving the proceeds from selling his flat**. You say the flat was sold **three months ago**. On that basis, the repayment date or event has likely already passed.

If the exact wording in the messages is clear, that will help. For example, messages that show:

Where a loan is proved, the lender may generally claim the **outstanding principal**, less sums already repaid. If there was no agreed contractual interest, the court may still award interest in some circumstances.

**Application:**  
If the original loan was **S$25,000** and he has repaid **S$2,000**, the apparent outstanding sum is **S$23,000**.

**Conclusion:**  
Your likely principal claim is **S$23,000**, subject to proof of the loan and the S$2,000 repayment.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Is the contractor likely in breach?

**Issue:**  
The contract price was **S$48,000**, completion was required within **10 weeks**, and after **16 weeks** the kitchen and electrical works are still unfinished.

**Rule:**  
As a general Singapore contract principle, where a contractor agrees to complete works by a specified date, failure to do so without lawful excuse is a breach. In construction matters, whether delay justifies termination depends on the seriousness of the breach and the contract terms. A contractor who fails to complete by the agreed date, and especially if the delay is substantial and the works remain materially incomplete, may be in repudiatory breach.  

In **Goh Eng Lee Andy v Yeo Jin Kow [2016] SGHC 110**, the court held that a contractor who failed to complete by the agreed completion date and later abandoned the works was in breach, and the employer was entitled to terminate and recover the additional completion cost.  

Where a party alleges that delay was caused by variations or employer conduct, that party generally bears the burden of proving the causal link. Similar reasoning appears in **Fongsoon Engineering (S) Pte Ltd v Kensteel Engineering Pte Ltd [2011] SGHC 82**.

**Application:**  
On your facts, there is a clear **6-week overrun** beyond the agreed 10-week period, and important parts of the works remain unfinished. That strongly suggests breach unless the contractor can show a valid basis for extension or that your conduct legally prevented completion on time.

The contractor says **verbal changes** caused delay. That argument is weaker where:
- the contract expressly says **variations must be agreed in writing**; and
- you say only **one S$1,500 variation** was approved by email.

If the contractor cannot prove written approval for the other alleged changes, it will be difficult for him to justify both the delay and the extra S$9,000 by relying on alleged verbal variations.

**Conclusion:**  
Based on the facts given, the contractor is **likely in breach** for failing to complete within the agreed period and for leaving material works unfinished.

### 2. Can the contractor claim S$9,000 extra for alleged verbal variations?

**Issue:**  
The contract says variations must be agreed **in writing**, but the contractor claims **S$9,000** extra based on alleged verbal changes.

**Rule:**  
Where a construction contract requires **written variation orders/approval**, claims for extra payment based only on verbal instructions are generally difficult to enforce. In **Fongsoon Engineering (S) Pte Ltd v Kensteel Engineering Pte Ltd [2011] SGHC 82**, the court rejected variation claims where the contract required written variation orders and none were produced.  

Similarly, in **Wei Siang Design Construction Pte Ltd v Euro Assets Holding (S) Pte Ltd (formerly known as Euro Search International Pte Ltd) [2018] SGHC 182**, the court treated unauthorised variations as generally not entitling the contractor to additional payment unless there was proper authority or later sanction.

**Application:**  
You have evidence of only **one written/email-approved variation for S$1,500**. On those facts:
- the contractor's claim for the additional **S$7,500** appears weak unless he can point to written approvals, or
- he can show that you clearly instructed and accepted extra works in a way that overrides the written-variation requirement.

Even if some verbal discussions occurred, that does not automatically entitle him to payment if the contract made written approval a condition and the alleged extra works were never properly authorised.

**Conclusion:**  
On the facts stated, the contractor is **unlikely to be entitled** to the full extra **S$9,000**. The only stronger item appears to be the **S$1,500 variation** you approved by email.

### 3. May you terminate the contractor?

**Issue:**  
You want to know whether you may terminate now.

**Rule:**  
Under Singapore law, termination at common law is generally available for **repudiation**, **fundamental breach**, or breach of a term sufficiently serious to justify ending the contract. This is reflected in **RDC Concrete Pte Ltd v Sato Kogyo (S) Pte Ltd [2007] 4 SLR(R) 413**.  

A delay does not automatically justify termination in every case. The question is whether the breach is sufficiently serious, viewed against the contract and the facts. A contractor's prolonged failure to complete substantial works, or conduct showing inability or unwillingness to finish, may justify termination.  

In **Goh Eng Lee Andy v Yeo Jin Kow [2016] SGHC 110**, failure to complete by the agreed date and subsequent abandonment entitled the owner to terminate.

**Application:**  
You likely have a **good basis to consider termination**, but it is best done carefully. Important factors include:
- whether the **10-week completion period** was expressed as firm rather than approximate;
- how substantial the unfinished works are;
- whether the contractor is still actively progressing works or has effectively stalled;
- whether you have already given a clear written demand for completion by a final reasonable deadline.

If you terminate too early or without sufficient basis, the contractor may argue that **you** wrongfully repudiated the contract. Because of that risk, the safest course is usually to first send a formal written notice:
- recording the delay,
- rejecting unapproved variations,
- requiring completion of specified outstanding works by a short final deadline, and
- stating that failing compliance, you will treat the contract as terminated and engage others to complete.

**Conclusion:**  
You **may have grounds to terminate**, especially if the contractor is substantially delayed and cannot properly justify the delay. But termination should ideally follow a **clear final written notice**, unless the contractor has already plainly abandoned or refused to complete.

### 4. Can you recover the cost of another contractor finishing the works?

**Issue:**

**Rule:**  
If the original contractor is in breach and you validly terminate or are otherwise entitled to complete the works through others, you may generally claim the **reasonable additional cost of completion** as damages, subject to mitigation.  

In **Goh Eng Lee Andy v Yeo Jin Kow [2016] SGHC 110**, the court awarded the owner the additional cost incurred to complete the project with replacement contractors, applying ordinary contractual damages principles. The innocent party must take **reasonable steps to mitigate loss**.

**Application:**  
If the contractor is indeed in breach and you validly terminate, you may ordinarily claim:
- the **reasonable cost of engaging a replacement contractor** to complete the unfinished works;
- potentially the cost of rectifying defective work, if any;
- possibly other directly caused losses, if provable.

However, your recovery is usually based on **reasonable** completion cost, not simply any price quoted. So it helps if:
- the replacement quotation is commercially reasonable;
- the scope clearly matches the actual unfinished works;
- you obtain more than one quote if feasible;
- you keep proof that the original contractor was given a fair chance to finish.

Since you have already paid **80%**, that payment position will matter in the eventual accounting. **Conclusion:**  
Yes, **completion costs are in principle recoverable**, provided the contractor is in breach, your termination is justified, and your replacement cost is reasonable and properly documented.

### Issue 1: Was there a breach of contract?

**Rule**

Where a contract clearly identifies the event date, venue, capacity, package, and equipment, the supplier is ordinarily obliged to provide that agreed venue and services on the agreed date, unless a contractual excuse applies. Under Singapore law, contractual clauses are interpreted objectively and in context, with the court seeking the meaning a reasonable business person would understand from the agreement as a whole. A party cannot rely on a clause in a manner inconsistent with its commercial purpose or rewrite the bargain after the event.

**Application**

On your facts, the contract appears sufficiently certain and complete: the date, room, capacity, catering package, and audiovisual equipment were all agreed. The hotel did not merely propose an adjustment; it said the ballroom was no longer available because another booking was preferred.

The hotel’s later description of the situation as an **“operational-security requirement outside its control”** points the other way, but a court would examine the surrounding documents objectively. If the real reason was that the hotel voluntarily displaced your booking to accommodate a more valuable customer, the hotel is unlikely to be excused.

**Conclusion**

Your **primary case on breach is strong**, subject to what the actual force-majeure wording says and what contemporaneous documents show about the real reason for cancellation.

### Issue 2: Does the force-majeure wording likely protect the hotel?

**Rule**

A force-majeure clause depends entirely on its wording. The court construes it objectively, in context, and according to commercial purpose. Clauses triggered where performance is **“prevented by events beyond reasonable control”** are generally read more narrowly than clauses referring to events that merely **hinder**, **delay**, or make performance less profitable.

A party usually cannot rely on such a clause if:
- the event was not truly outside its control;
- performance remained possible but became less attractive commercially; or
- the party effectively caused the problem itself by choosing to prioritise another obligation.

The fact that a government-linked conference wanted the whole venue does not by itself mean performance was “prevented”. The key question is whether the hotel **had to** displace your booking because of a genuine external requirement, or **chose to** do so for commercial or relationship reasons.

**Application**

There are two competing narratives:

| Hotel narrative | Your likely response |
|---|---|
| A security or operational requirement tied to a government-linked event made the ballroom unavailable, and this was outside its reasonable control. | The hotel’s own earlier email says the booking was “commercially too important to decline”, suggesting choice rather than compulsion. |
| The whole venue had to be controlled for security reasons that could not reasonably be accommodated around your event. | The hotel must show the actual requirement: who imposed it, when, on what terms, and whether partial operation of the venue was prohibited. |
| The alternative smaller room or new date was reasonable. | The substitute was not like-for-like because your contracted capacity was about 310 and the substitute held only 180, so it did not preserve the benefit of the bargain. |

If the hotel was **required by an external authority** to make the ballroom unavailable, and had no realistic ability to perform, it has a better argument. If instead the government-linked conference simply insisted on exclusivity and the hotel agreed because the business was too important to lose, that is usually still a **voluntary commercial decision**.

The phrase **“prevented by events beyond its reasonable control”** is helpful to you because “prevented” ordinarily requires real impossibility or something close to it, not mere inconvenience or better business opportunities.

**Conclusion**

On the facts presently stated, the hotel’s reliance on force majeure looks **contestable and probably weak**, especially because of the “commercially too important to decline” message. The hotel’s best defence would depend on proving a genuine external operational or security mandate that left it with no practical choice.

### Issue 3: Are the limitation clauses likely valid, and how far do they go?

**Rule**

Under Singapore law, limitation and exclusion clauses are generally enforceable in commercial contracts if properly incorporated and clearly worded. However:

1. They are construed **strictly**, especially where a party seeks to exclude liability for its own breach.
2. Any ambiguity is usually read against the party relying on the clause.
3. A clause that limits liability if the hotel **“cancels”** may or may not extend to a cancellation that was itself **wrongful** and outside the force-majeure clause; wording matters.
4. An exclusion of **loss of profit** may bar claims for net profit, anticipated revenue margin, or similar expectation-based profit claims.
5. An exclusion of **consequential loss** in Singapore commercial practice is often read as excluding losses falling within the **second limb** type of remoteness analysis, rather than all direct loss. It does **not automatically exclude all losses flowing from breach**.

If applicable statutory controls on unfair contract terms are engaged, they may affect enforceability, but in a negotiated business-to-business event contract, the hotel often starts from a stronger position than in a consumer context.

**Application**

There are two important limitation provisions here:

#### A. Refund-only clause
You say the contract allows the hotel to cancel if performance is prevented by events beyond reasonable control, and **if it cancels its liability is limited to refunding sums paid**.

The key interpretive question is whether that refund-only protection applies:
- **only when the cancellation falls within the force-majeure clause**, or
- to **any cancellation by the hotel**, including a wrongful one.

A court is likely to read the clause in context. If the structure is: “hotel may cancel if prevented by events beyond reasonable control; if it cancels liability is limited to refund”, the better reading is often that the refund cap is tied to **that excused category of cancellation**, not any deliberate breach for commercial reasons.

If the hotel’s cancellation was not truly within the clause, you have a respectable argument that the refund-only cap **does not apply**.

If, however, the clause is drafted broadly and independently — for example, “in any event if the hotel cancels for any reason its sole liability is refund” — the hotel’s position improves materially.

#### B. Exclusion of loss of profit and consequential loss
This clause is more likely to be effective, even if the hotel was in breach, provided it is clearly drafted and incorporated.

It likely gives the hotel a **good defence** to:
- claims for lost profit on the event;
- claims based on projected ticketing margin;
- some reputational or downstream business losses;
- possibly losses characterised as consequential or special, depending on wording.

But it may **not** exclude direct wasted expenditure or direct replacement costs, unless the drafting clearly says so.

**Conclusion**

- Your best argument is that the **refund-only clause does not protect a wrongful commercial cancellation** falling outside force majeure.
- The **loss of profit / consequential loss exclusion** is more likely to be upheld and is a serious obstacle to speculative revenue-based claims.
- The precise drafting and layout of the contract will be critical.

### Issue 4: Causation, remoteness, and mitigation

**Rule**

To recover damages, you must show:
1. the loss was **caused by** the breach;
2. the loss was not too **remote**; and
3. you took reasonable steps to **mitigate** it.

Loss is generally recoverable if it arises naturally from the breach or was within the reasonable contemplation of the parties when the contract was made. You cannot recover loss that is too speculative or that could reasonably have been avoided.

**Application**

#### Causation
The cancellation on 12 August appears to be the factual trigger for:
- the extra venue cost;
- redesign costs caused by moving the event;
- some attendee refunds due to changed timing;
- possibly some wasted marketing spend if it had to be redone;
- possibly some speaker fees if those became wasted or duplicated because of the venue change.

These are causation-positive heads, but each must still be tested for remoteness and mitigation.

#### Remoteness
The more foreseeable a loss is in the context of an event-venue contract, the stronger the claim.

Likely foreseeable:
- the need to book a replacement venue at higher cost on relatively short notice;
- costs of revising event materials after changing venue/date;
- some attendee refunds if the event had to be rescheduled.

More arguable:
- non-refundable speaker fees, depending on whether they were entirely wasted or whether the speakers still attended the rearranged event;
- duplicated marketing costs, if you had to rerun marketing because of the move.

More vulnerable:
- lost projected ticket revenue, especially where sales had not closed and the event still went ahead;
- net profit claims;
- broad business opportunity or reputational claims.

#### Mitigation
You did obtain a replacement venue and proceed with the event. That generally supports mitigation. The replacement venue cost of **S$52,000** versus the original **S$36,000** gives an additional **S$16,000**. On the face of it, that looks reasonable if it was the nearest suitable venue for a 310-person event on short notice.

Rejecting the smaller room also appears reasonable because it would not accommodate your sold demand. A court is unlikely to require you to accept a substitute that materially reduces capacity and undermines the event.

The hotel may still argue:
- you should have chosen a cheaper alternative;
- some redesign or marketing costs were excessive;
- the revised time/date, rather than the venue change itself, caused part of the attendee refund loss;
- any shortfall in revenue is partly due to ordinary sales uncertainty.

**Conclusion**

Your claims for direct replacement and adaptation costs have the best causation and mitigation profile. Revenue-based claims are materially weaker.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Prima facie defamation risk

A defamation claim generally requires the claimant to show:
- the statement is **defamatory**,
- it **refers** to the claimant, and
- it was **published** to at least one other person.

A statement is defamatory if it tends to lower the claimant in the estimation of right-thinking members of society, cause the claimant to be shunned or avoided, or expose it to hatred, contempt or ridicule. An accusation that a business “cheats parents” is potentially serious because it suggests **dishonest business conduct**.

Where the statement concerns a business, the key question is whether the words would make ordinary readers think worse of that business in its trade or reputation. On the facts you gave, the tuition centre may have an arguable claim.

That principle is reflected in **Ting Jun Heng v Yap Kok Hua & Anor [2020] SGHC 211**.

Where a pedestrian is crossing with the pedestrian signal in his or her favour, the driver will usually face substantial difficulty avoiding primary liability if the collision occurred while the pedestrian was already on the crossing. However, that does **not** mean every pedestrian is automatically partly at fault merely because a collision happened on a crossing. The court in **Li Jianlin v Asnah bte AB Rahman [2014] SGHC 198** found no contributory negligence where the pedestrian was crossing with the lights in his favour and the driver’s red-light breach was the true cause of the accident.

## Analysis of Legal Issues

### 1. Negligence claim against the rider

**Issue:**  
Whether the rider is likely liable in negligence for colliding into you from behind on a shared path.

**Rule:**  
Road users, including cyclists, must keep a proper lookout and take reasonable care for the safety of more vulnerable users. Singapore decisions recognise that cyclists are expected to ride carefully, keep a proper course, and avoid unsafe manoeuvres. Motorists and other road users must also take precautions when approaching vulnerable users and cannot rely on minimal warnings alone if the circumstances call for more. In **Lyu Yongqiang v Yu Mau Hing and another [2015] SGHC 200**, the court emphasised the need to give vulnerable road users adequate space and to approach them with sufficient care. In **Chua Kee Lam (next friend to Chua Peck Seng) v Moksha and Another [2009] SGHC 110**, the court held that merely sounding a warning is not enough if further precautionary steps should have been taken. In **Hapsuwan Sakon v CT Civil Construction Pte Ltd & Anor [2017] SGHC 63**, failure to keep a proper lookout and failure to take evasive action supported a finding of negligence.

**Application:**  
On your facts, the case against the rider appears **strong**:

- You were **walking**, not cycling or driving.
- The rider came **from behind**, which usually means he had the better opportunity to observe, slow down, steer away, or warn.
- There is a witness saying he was **looking down at the app immediately before impact**. If proved, that strongly supports breach of duty.
- The rider’s own statement that the app had just assigned a second order within a tight time window may help explain distraction and haste, though it does not excuse careless riding.
- The injuries you describe — fracture, concussion, surgery, prolonged medical leave and possible long-term restriction — are plainly the sort of physical harm that is reasonably foreseeable from a cyclist colliding with a pedestrian. Under the remoteness principles reflected in **Fong Maun Yee v Yoong Weng Ho Robert [1997] 1 SLR(R) 751**, **Ho Soo Fong v Standard Chartered Bank [2007] 2 SLR(R) 181**, and **Ngiam Kong Seng v Lim Chiew Hock [2008] 3 SLR(R) 674**, personal injury of this general type is not too remote.

**Conclusion:**  
You likely have a **good negligence claim against the rider**.

### 2. Contributory negligence risks

**Issue:**

**Rule:**  
A claimant’s damages may be reduced if they failed to take reasonable care for their own safety. Singapore cases show that contributory negligence depends heavily on whether the claimant had a realistic opportunity to avoid the collision. In **Li Jianlin v Asnah bte AB Rahman [2014] SGHC 198**, the pedestrian was found **not contributorily negligent** because he was using a crossing properly and could reasonably expect vehicles to comply. By contrast, in **Hapsuwan Sakon v CT Civil Construction Pte Ltd & Anor [2017] SGHC 63**, the cyclist was heavily contributorily negligent for failing to keep a proper lookout. In **Cai Xiao Qing v Leow Fa Dong (by his next friend Leow Chye Huat) [2012] SGHC 67**, blame was apportioned where a cyclist moved into danger without sufficient regard for oncoming traffic. In **Lyu Yongqiang v Yu Mau Hing and another [2015] SGHC 200**, the cyclist still bore some responsibility because he could have stopped and let the bus pass.

**Application:**  
Possible allegations against you may include:

- walking unpredictably;
- suddenly changing direction;
- using headphones or looking at a phone;
- occupying the path in a way that obstructed the rider;
- failing to keep to one side of the shared path.

Your **contributory negligence risk appears low on the present facts**, though it will depend on CCTV, witness evidence, path layout, lighting, and whether you made any sudden movement.
