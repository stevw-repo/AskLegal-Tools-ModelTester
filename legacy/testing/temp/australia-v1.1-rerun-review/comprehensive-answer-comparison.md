## Documents compared

- [Original answers — au-prompt-v1.0.pdf](./au-prompt-v1.0.pdf)
- [Revised answers — au-prompt-v1.1.pdf](./au-prompt-v1.1.pdf)

**Assessment date:** 29 July 2026  
**Scope:** Comparative evaluation of the legal accuracy, strengths, weaknesses, practical safety, and overall soundness of the 18 answer pairs in the two PDFs.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Overall finding:** The revised answers are better presented, but they are not reliably sound legal advice. The revision improves structure, issue identification, and cautious language in places, but it does not repair several serious failures involving controlling legislation, obsolete law, deadlines, jurisdiction, forum selection, and binding precedent.

## Executive conclusion

- Version 1.1 has clearer issue headings, more disciplined jurisdictional framing, and generally better action-plan language.
- It makes tangible progress in approximately five answers: **au-002, au-006, au-007, au-011, and au-012**.
- It does not repair the most serious errors in **au-003, au-008, au-010, au-015, or au-016**.
- Only **au-013** is substantively robust.
- **au-017** and **au-018** are safe mainly because they appropriately request the missing jurisdiction rather than attempting a definitive answer.
- The remaining answers range from materially incomplete to affirmatively unsafe.
- The increase from 99 pages to 104 pages largely represents more explanation, not a corresponding increase in legal reliability.

## Rating key

| Rating | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sound** | The central legal framework and conclusion are substantially correct, subject to ordinary fact verification. |
| **Directionally sound but incomplete** | The broad conclusion is plausible, but material legislation, procedure, remedies, jurisdictional gateways, or deadlines are missing. |
| **Unsafe / not sound** | A material error or omission could mislead the user, cause use of the wrong legal regime, or jeopardise substantive or procedural rights. |

### au-001 — Remote employee, dismissal, workers’ compensation, and workplace safety

#### Original v1.0

**Strengths**

#### Revised v1.1

## Comparative weaknesses and systemic defects

### 1. Operative legislation is repeatedly missing

The answers often cite background, definition, application, or jurisdiction provisions while omitting the provision that actually:

- creates the duty;
- prohibits the conduct;
- supplies the cause of action;
- establishes the remedy;
- imposes the deadline; or
- gives the court or tribunal jurisdiction.

Examples include:

- Fair Work Act provisions in au-001 and au-005;
- EPBC Act provisions in au-003;
- Privacy Act Part IIIC in au-004;
- defamation serious-harm, concerns-notice, and limitation provisions in au-006;
- Queensland BIF payment-claim and adjudication provisions in au-012;
- Corporations Act duties and remedies in au-015.

### 2. Currentness checking has failed

Objective warning signs include:

- the repealed Queensland Property Law Act 1974 in au-008;
- the obsolete NT workers’ compensation title in au-007;
- the former “Migration and Refugee Division” terminology in au-010;
- failure to identify Victoria’s current psychological-health regulations in au-001.

### 3. More authorities do not necessarily mean better authority

Several answers contain long lists of cases but omit the controlling statute. This creates an appearance of depth without supplying the legal rule needed to answer the question.

au-001 is a particularly clear example: numerous workers’ compensation authorities do not replace applying the Fair Work Act and Victorian OHS legislation.

### 4. Deadline handling remains dangerous

The revised answers frequently use:

- “usually short”;
- “act promptly”;
- a generic tribunal period; or
- no deadline at all.

This is not safe where a statutory time limit may extinguish or seriously impair a right. Important examples include:

- dismissal and general-protections applications;
- discrimination complaints;
- defamation claims and concerns notices;
- de facto property applications;
- migration review;
- security-of-payment adjudication;
- personal-injury claims.

### 5. Cross-jurisdictional questions are incompletely researched

The response should test every jurisdiction raised by the facts. It instead omits entire regimes in:

- au-003 — Commonwealth EPBC law;
- au-004 — Tasmanian privacy law;
- au-007 — Queensland and NSW workers’ compensation comparison;
- au-011 — complete territorial and overlap analysis.

### 6. Forum conclusions are sometimes asserted rather than established

Naming QCAT, a court, or a tribunal is not enough. A legally useful answer must establish:

- subject-matter jurisdiction;
- territorial connection;
- monetary limit;
- standing;
- reviewability;
- applicable filing procedure; and
- any contractual forum or exclusive-jurisdiction issue.

### 7. Precedent methodology remains defective

au-016 shows that the system does not reliably retrieve or apply the controlling authority even when the question directly tests a well-known precedent rule.

The answer’s invitation to choose the “most persuasive” interstate appellate decision is inconsistent with the *Farah* rule unless the relevant authority can properly be distinguished or is considered plainly wrong.

### 8. Conclusions are often more confident than their authorities permit

A disclaimer does not cure a definitive conclusion based on:

- missing legislation;
- obsolete legislation;
- an unverified deadline;
- an unproved forum gateway; or
- an incomplete jurisdictional comparison.

### 9. Practical plans are not consistently tied to legal consequences

Action plans should explain why each step matters. For example:

- preserve the limitation period;
- satisfy a mandatory precondition;
- prevent asset dissipation;
- comply with an adjudication deadline;
- establish standing;
- preserve evidence;
- avoid inconsistent positions or double recovery.

Several answers instead provide generic document lists that are sensible but not legally prioritised.

## Prompt-side and validation implications

The results indicate that future prompt revisions should impose mandatory completion gates rather than merely request a more thorough discussion.

### Required validation gates

Before finalising an answer, the system should be required to confirm:

1. **Current law**
   - The cited Act is currently in force.
   - Its title is current.
   - Relevant commencement, repeal, replacement, and transitional provisions have been checked.

2. **Operative provisions**
   - Every material conclusion is supported by the provision that creates the right, duty, prohibition, remedy, deadline, or jurisdiction.

3. **All jurisdictions raised**
   - Every Commonwealth, state, or territory regime expressly or implicitly raised by the facts has been tested.

4. **Exact deadline treatment**
   - The answer must give the exact sourced deadline and trigger.
   - If the deadline cannot safely be established, it must say so expressly and give an urgent referral warning.
   - Generic periods must not be supplied as substitutes.

5. **Forum gateway**
   - The answer must establish why the named court, tribunal, or review body has jurisdiction.

6. **Remedies and preconditions**
   - Mandatory notice, conference, complaint, review, leave, or adjudication steps must be identified.

7. **Controlling precedent**
   - The system must search for and apply controlling High Court and relevant appellate authority.
   - It must distinguish binding rules, seriously considered dicta, persuasive authority, and distinguishable cases.

8. **Internal contradiction check**
   - The analysis must not apply one jurisdiction’s substantive test after concluding that another jurisdiction governs.

9. **Confidence calibration**
   - Definitive language should be prohibited where an essential fact, statutory gateway, or source remains unverified.

10. **Action-plan prioritisation**
    - Urgent and rights-preserving steps must appear first.
    - Each step should be tied to its legal purpose and deadline.

## Final assessment

The revised answers show that presentation-level prompt changes worked, but substantive legal-quality controls did not. The central defect is not simply insufficient detail. It is the absence of a dependable process for:

- identifying the controlling regime;
- checking that the law is current;
- retrieving the operative provisions;
- applying the correct jurisdictional and procedural gateways;
- protecting deadlines; and
- verifying conclusions against binding authority.

Until those controls are implemented and the affected answers are regenerated and independently checked, the advice should be treated as a draft research aid rather than advice suitable for publication or reliance.
